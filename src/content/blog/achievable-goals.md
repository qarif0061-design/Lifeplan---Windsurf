The A in SMART is where many plans quietly break. People write a clear, measurable target, then set a bar so high that life never catches up. **Achievable SMART goals** fix that: they keep the ambition, but they match the target to your time, skill and resources. This guide explains what "achievable" means, how it differs from "realistic," and how to test and rewrite any goal so you can actually finish it.

If you want the full five-letter framework first, start with our [complete guide to SMART goals](/articles/smart-goals-complete-guide).

## What does achievable mean in SMART goals?

An achievable goal is one you can reasonably reach with the time, skills, tools and support you have (or can get) before the deadline. It stretches you a little. It does not assume a perfect week, unlimited money or a brand-new skill you have never practiced.

A quick test: if you explained the goal to a friend who knows your schedule, would they say "tough but doable" or "only if everything goes perfectly"? Achievable goals live in the first bucket.

Compare these:

- **Not achievable (for most people):** "Launch a six-product online store, hit 10,000 subscribers and learn video editing, all in 30 days, while working a full-time job."
- **Achievable:** "Publish one digital product and get the first 20 email subscribers in 30 days, using two focused evenings per week."

Same direction. Different odds of finishing.

"Attainable goals" means the same thing in everyday language. Some SMART versions use "Attainable" instead of "Achievable." The idea does not change: can you get there from where you stand today?

## Achievable vs realistic: what's the difference?

People often mix the two. In many SMART versions, **A = Achievable (or Attainable)** and **R = Relevant**. In other versions, people say "Realistic" for the A. That is why "achievable vs realistic" shows up in so many searches.

Here is a practical way to keep them separate:

- **Achievable / attainable** answers: *Can I do this with my current (or soon-available) capacity?*
- **Relevant** answers: *Does this goal matter for my bigger priorities?*
- **Realistic** (when people use that word) usually overlaps with achievable: *Is this grounded in real constraints, not wishful thinking?*

So if your version of SMART uses Realistic for A, treat it like achievable. If your version uses Relevant for R, keep relevance as a separate check. You can read more examples of grounded targets in our [realistic SMART goals examples](/articles/realistic-smart-goals-examples).

A goal can be relevant but not achievable ("I want a promotion next month with no open roles"). It can also be achievable but not relevant ("I can run a 5K in eight weeks, but my real priority this quarter is finishing a certification"). Both checks matter.

## Why the A in SMART gets ignored

Specific and Measurable feel easy to write. Time-bound is a date on a calendar. Achievable asks for honesty about capacity, and honesty is harder.

Common ways the A gets skipped:

1. **Copying someone else's pace.** A creator posts "I wrote a book in 90 days." Your schedule is not theirs.
2. **Planning for perfect weeks.** You plan 10 focused hours, then meetings, kids, travel and fatigue show up.
3. **Stacking too many firsts.** New skill + new tool + new audience + tight deadline is usually one first too many.
4. **Confusing motivation with capacity.** Feeling fired up on Sunday night is not the same as having Tuesday and Thursday free.

When the A is weak, you do not usually quit on day one. You slip in week three, feel behind, and stop checking the goal. Fixing achievability early protects the rest of the SMART criteria. For a reminder of all five letters, see [SMART criteria for goals](/articles/smart-criteria).

## How to test if a goal is achievable (5 checks)

Use these five checks before you lock a target. If two or more fail, rewrite the goal.

### 1. Time check
List the hours per week you can truly protect. Multiply by the number of weeks until the deadline. That is your capacity budget. Then estimate the hours the goal needs. If the goal needs 40 hours and you have 16, it is not achievable as written.

### 2. Skill check
Name the skills required. Mark each as "have," "can learn fast," or "need training." One "need training" item is fine if the deadline includes learning time. Three "need training" items usually means the goal is too big for this window.

### 3. Resource check
Money, tools, access, and other people count. "Publish a course" needs recording tools and a place to host it. "Run a half marathon" needs a training plan and recovery time. Missing resources do not kill the goal; they change the date or the scope.

### 4. Dependency check
Who else has to say yes? Approvals, clients, partners and family schedules create delays you do not control. Achievable goals either remove those dependencies or build buffer for them.

### 5. History check
Look at your last similar attempt. If you planned five workouts a week and averaged two, an achievable next goal starts near two and builds up, not at five again.

Write the answers down. A goal that survives all five checks is usually an achievable SMART goal worth committing to.

## How to rewrite a stretch goal into an achievable one

You do not have to dump a big dream. You shrink the *next* target so progress is real.

**Pattern 1: Cut the scope**
- Before: "Redesign the whole website this month."
- After: "Redesign the homepage and pricing page this month; schedule the rest for next month."

**Pattern 2: Extend the deadline**
- Before: "Reach conversational Spanish in 6 weeks."
- After: "Complete units 1–4 of my course and hold four 15-minute practice chats in 8 weeks."

**Pattern 3: Reduce the frequency**
- Before: "Write every day for 60 minutes."
- After: "Write four mornings a week for 25 minutes, tracked in my planner."

**Pattern 4: Separate learning from performing**
- Before: "Launch paid ads profitably in two weeks with no ad experience."
- After: "Complete one beginner ads course and run one $50 test campaign in four weeks, then review results before scaling."

**Pattern 5: Keep the dream as a long-term goal**
Park the big vision as a one-year or three-year goal. Set a 30–90 day achievable SMART goal that moves you toward it. The [measurable goals guide](/articles/measurable-goals-guide) shows how to attach a clear number once the scope is realistic.

## Achievable goal examples (before and after)

### Work
- Before: "Become the top salesperson on the team this quarter."
- After: "Increase my monthly closed deals from 6 to 8 by the end of the quarter, with two extra outreach blocks each week."

### Study
- Before: "Get an A in every subject."
- After: "Raise my weakest subject from C to B this term by completing two past papers every weekend."

### Fitness
- Before: "Go from couch to marathon in 10 weeks."
- After: "Complete a couch-to-5K plan in 10 weeks, running three times per week."

### Money
- Before: "Save $10,000 in three months on my current salary with no plan."
- After: "Save $1,200 in three months by moving $100 to savings on each payday."

### Personal projects
- Before: "Build and launch a full app alone in 30 days."
- After: "Ship a working prototype of the core feature in 30 days, then plan launch for the next cycle."

Notice each "after" version still asks for progress. It just matches capacity. That is the heart of achievable SMART goals.

## A simple weekly routine to keep goals achievable

Achievability is not a one-time edit. Life changes, so review weekly.

1. **Sunday (10 minutes):** Look at next week's calendar. Block the hours your goal needs. If the week is packed, shrink this week's milestone instead of pretending.
2. **Midweek (5 minutes):** Check whether you used the blocked time. If not, reschedule one block — do not wait for "later."
3. **Friday (5 minutes):** Mark progress against the number. If you are behind for two weeks in a row, rewrite the target with the five checks above.

## FAQ: achievable SMART goals

### What does achievable mean in SMART goals?
It means you can reasonably reach the goal with the time, skills, tools and support available before the deadline. The goal should stretch you, but it should not depend on a perfect schedule or resources you cannot get.

### What is an achievable goal example?
"Walk 8,000 steps on at least five days each week for the next eight weeks, tracked on my phone" is an achievable goal for many beginners. "Walk 20,000 steps every day starting tomorrow" usually is not, unless that already matches your current habit.

### Achievable vs realistic — are they the same?
Often yes in casual use. In many SMART models, A means Achievable or Attainable, and R means Relevant. When people say "realistic," they usually mean the same honesty check as achievable: the goal fits real constraints. Keep Relevant as its own question about priorities.

### How do I know if my goal is too hard?
Run the five checks: time, skill, resources, dependencies and history. If your required hours exceed protected hours, or if you need several new skills at once with no learning buffer, the goal is too hard for this window. Shrink scope, extend the date or split the work.

### Can an achievable goal still be ambitious?
Yes. Ambition belongs in the direction and the long-term vision. Achievability belongs in the next deadline. A strong system stacks achievable wins toward a bigger outcome, instead of betting everything on one oversized sprint.

### Should I lower the goal every time I miss it?
Not every miss. First ask whether the week was unusual (travel, illness) or whether the plan was wrong. After two normal weeks of missing, rewrite the goal. Constant lowering without a new plan becomes avoidance; honest rewriting is strategy.
