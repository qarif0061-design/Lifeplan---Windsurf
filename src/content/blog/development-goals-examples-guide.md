"I need some development goals" is a request that comes up in appraisals, promotion conversations and personal plans, and it often leads to a blank page. The phrase is broad on purpose, which is why it's hard to start. This guide gives you **development goals examples** for career, skills, leadership and personal growth, written so you can adapt the numbers and dates to your own role.

A development goal is any goal that builds a capability you'll still have after the project or year is over: a skill, a habit, a qualification or a way of working. It is different from a delivery goal, which is about a result you ship. You usually need both.

## What a good development goal looks like
A useful development goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. In practice that means:

- it names the skill or behaviour, not a personality change,
- it has a number, a milestone or other evidence you can show,
- it fits a real week, not a fantasy week,
- it connects to a reason that matters in your work or life,
- it has a date and a review point.

**Weak:** "Get better at presenting."
**Strong:** "Deliver a 10-minute update at the monthly team meeting for the next six months, practise once the week before, and collect feedback after the third and sixth sessions."

The [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) formula will help you write the sentence: *I will [practice] [how often] by [date], so that [reason], and I'll track it by [evidence].*

## Development goals examples: skills and knowledge
1. Complete a recognised course in a tool I use at work (spreadsheets, a language, a design or data tool) by 30 September, studying three hours a week, and finish with one small project I can show.
2. Learn the five most useful features of our main software within 8 weeks, and run a 15-minute walkthrough for colleagues afterwards.
3. Read one professional book a month for six months and share a half-page summary with my team after each one.
4. Earn a certification in my field by 30 November, with a practice exam done by 30 September.
5. Spend 45 focused minutes, twice a week for 12 weeks, practising a skill I will need in the next role I want.

## Development goals examples: career and role growth
6. Have a 30-minute career conversation with my manager or a mentor once a quarter this year, with two questions prepared each time.
7. Take the lead on one small project from plan to review by 30 June, and write a short note on what I learned.
8. Build a simple portfolio of three pieces of work by 31 August, each with the problem, what I did and the result.
9. Shadow a senior colleague for two hours a month for six months, and write one lesson after each session.
10. Prepare a promotion or progression case by 31 October, with five documented achievements and a conversation booked the month before.

## Development goals examples: leadership and people skills
You don't need a manager title to set these.

11. Speak at least once in every team meeting for the next 10 meetings, even if it's a short update or a question.
12. Give useful written feedback to a colleague at least twice a month for the next quarter.
13. Mentor a new starter for 12 weeks, meeting for 20 minutes each week and tracking their onboarding checklist.
14. Facilitate one meeting or workshop a month for six months, and ask for feedback from three people each time.
15. Practise a difficult conversation (a request, a boundary, or a piece of feedback) once a week for eight weeks, and note what happened afterwards.

## Development goals examples: personal growth
Development isn't only for work.

16. Practise a language for 15 minutes on five days a week for 12 weeks, plus one short conversation at the weekend.
17. Write 300 words of a personal project on three days a week for two months.
18. Complete a beginner course in a hobby or practical skill by the end of the term, attending or practising once a week.
19. Read 10 pages a day for 60 days, and keep a list of finished books.
20. Join a club or class and attend at least six sessions in the next three months, to meet people and practise a skill outside work.

## How to choose the right development goal
If the list feels long, don't start with the most impressive item. Use three filters:

- **Need:** What skill, if I had it in six months, would make the rest of my work or life easier?
- **Use:** Will I have a real chance to practise it soon, or will it sit unused?
- **Fit:** Can the practice land in a normal week, not a perfect one?

Pick one goal, or two at most. A short list you review will move more than a long list you ignore. If several options feel urgent, [how to prioritize goals](/articles/how-to-prioritize-goals) can help you choose.


A development goal also works better when you can point to a real use for it in the next few months. "Learn a tool we might use someday" is harder to keep than "learn the tool I'll need for the project that starts in May." If no use is coming, create one: a short internal talk, a practice project, or a documented improvement in your current work. The practice gives the learning somewhere to go.

Then write the weekly practice before you write the finish line. "Complete the course by 30 September" is the outcome. "Study three hours a week, Tuesday and Thursday evenings" is what makes it happen.

## How to review development goals
Development is easy to postpone because there is usually no client deadline. A simple rhythm keeps it honest:

- **Weekly (10 minutes):** Did the practice happen? If not, shrink the session, don't abandon the goal.
- **Monthly:** Is the evidence growing (modules finished, sessions delivered, pages written)?
- **End of the cycle:** Keep the habit, raise the target, or pick a new skill.

## Common mistakes
- **Goals about who you are, not what you'll do.** "Be more confident" needs a behaviour, such as speaking once in each meeting.
- **No place to practise.** A course with no project, or a leadership goal with no meeting to lead, tends to fade.
- **Too many at once.** Two is plenty.
- **No evidence.** If you can't show what changed, the review becomes a vague conversation.
- **Waiting for a quiet month.** Put a small session in the calendar now. Quiet months are rare.

## FAQ: development goals examples
### What are some good development goals examples?
Good examples name a skill, a practice and a date. Completing a course with a small project by 30 September, delivering a short update at every team meeting for six months, or mentoring a new starter for 12 weeks are all solid starting points.

### How many development goals should I have?
One or two at a time. Development goals compete with delivery work. A small number you actually practise each week will teach you more than a long list written for a form.

### How do I write a development goal for a performance review?
Start with the skill you need, add a weekly practice, a measure or piece of evidence, and a date. Link it to a team or role need so your manager can support it. Then keep a short log of what you did, so the review is based on evidence.

### How do I measure personal or leadership development?
Measure the behaviour, not the feeling. Count practice sessions, talks given, feedback conversations, courses finished or projects led. Confidence and "presence" often follow the practice, but they make a poor weekly metric.
