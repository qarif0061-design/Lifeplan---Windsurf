Engineers are used to requirements, metrics and deadlines, but personal and team goals still come out vague: "improve code quality," "be more proactive," "ship faster." Those phrases are hard to review and easy to argue about. **Engineering SMART goals examples** show how to turn the same intentions into targets you can measure, schedule and discuss in a one-to-one or a sprint review.

This guide gives examples for delivery, quality, reliability, design work, collaboration and professional development. The numbers are illustrations. Use your own baselines, and follow your organisation's safety, quality and compliance rules. For a shorter role-specific list, see the [engineering SMART goals examples](/articles/engineering-smart-goals-examples) page, and for mechanical roles, [SMART goals for mechanical engineers](/articles/smart-goals-for-mechanical-engineers-examples).

## Why SMART goals work well in engineering
Engineering work already produces numbers: cycle time, defect counts, uptime, test coverage, review turnaround, on-time delivery. The SMART method (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) simply makes sure a goal uses those numbers honestly.

A good engineering goal:

- names a system, process or skill, not a slogan,
- starts from a baseline,
- has a target and a date,
- is something the person or team can actually influence,
- is reviewed on a fixed rhythm, not only at the end of the year.

Use this formula: *In [area], I/we will [change] so that [measure] moves from [baseline] to [target] by [date], by doing [practice].* The [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide has more templates.

## Engineering SMART goals examples: delivery and planning
1. Reduce the average time from ticket start to merge from 8 days to 5 days by the end of Q2, by breaking work into smaller stories and reviewing blockers in the twice-weekly stand-up.
2. Raise the percentage of work delivered in the planned sprint from 70% to 85% over the next 4 sprints, measured at each sprint review.
3. Cut the number of tickets that stay in "in progress" for more than 5 days from 12 a month to 4 a month by 30 June, by flagging them in the Monday planning meeting.
4. Provide a written estimate range (best case, likely, worst case) for every new project I lead this year, and compare the result with the estimate at close-out.
5. Reduce carry-over work at the end of each month from 30% of planned items to 15% within two quarters, by limiting work in progress.

## Engineering SMART goals examples: quality, reliability and safety
6. Reduce escaped defects reported in the first 30 days after release from 10 to 5 per quarter by 30 September, by adding a pre-release checklist and a weekly defect review.
7. Increase automated test coverage on the service I own from 55% to 70% by 31 July, adding tests in two focused sessions a week.
8. Bring mean time to recover from a Sev-2 incident from 4 hours to 2 hours by 31 December, by writing runbooks for the top 5 failure modes and rehearsing one of them each month.
9. Close 90% of review comments within two working days for the next 12 weeks, tracked on the team's review board.
10. Complete the required safety or quality training for my role by 31 May, and apply one practice from it in the next design review, with a short note in the project file.

Quality goals should be paired with a method, not only a target. "Fewer bugs" without a change in practice usually means "hope."

## Engineering SMART goals examples: design and technical work
11. Produce a one-page design note before starting any piece of work estimated at more than 3 days, for the next 6 months, and file it where the team can find it.
12. Reduce rework after design review by 25% in two quarters, measured by the number of review cycles per project, by circulating the draft 48 hours before the meeting.
13. Improve documentation for the three most-used internal tools by 30 June, with a worked example and a troubleshooting section for each.
14. Shadow a senior engineer on two design reviews a month for 6 months, and write down one lesson after each one.
15. Deliver a short technical talk (20 minutes) to the team once a quarter this year, with feedback collected from at least 5 colleagues.

## Engineering SMART goals examples: collaboration and leadership
16. Give written feedback on a teammate's work within one business day, at least 8 times a month, for the next quarter.
17. Run a 30-minute retro after every project I lead, for the next 6 projects, and implement at least one agreed improvement each time.
18. Meet each person I mentor for 30 minutes every two weeks for the rest of the year, with a short written action after each session.
19. Share a weekly written status (done, next, risks) every Friday for the next 16 weeks, so stakeholders don't have to chase updates.
20. Facilitate one cross-team planning session a month for 6 months, and send a one-page summary of decisions the same day.

## Professional development goals for engineers
Technical careers stall when learning is left to "when things are quieter." Put a date on it.

21. Complete a course or certification in a tool or method we use (or will use next year) by 30 November, studying 3 hours a week, with a practice project done by 31 October.
22. Read one engineering book or long technical paper a month for 6 months, and share a half-page summary with the team.
23. Contribute to an internal or open-source repo at least twice a month for the next quarter, so I practise a codebase that isn't only my team's.
24. Prepare a promotion or progression case by 31 October, with 5 documented pieces of work and a conversation with my manager in the preceding month.
25. Learn the basics of the adjacent discipline I work with (for example, product, manufacturing, data or operations) by attending 4 structured sessions and writing a one-page note of how it changes my design decisions, by 30 June.

## How to set engineering goals without turning them into vanity metrics
- **Start with the baseline.** If you don't know today's defect count or review time, measure it for two to four weeks before you set a target.
- **Prefer measures the person can influence.** A junior engineer should not own a company-wide uptime number alone.
- **Pair a result with a practice.** The practice is what you do each week; the result tells you whether it helped.
- **Review in existing meetings.** Add a 5-minute look at the numbers to a sprint review or one-to-one, rather than creating a new reporting ritual.
- **Adjust when the system changes.** A re-org, a new product, or a hiring freeze can make last quarter's target unrealistic. Rewrite it on purpose. The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) and [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guides are useful here.

## Common mistakes
- **Goals that only measure activity.** "Attend 10 meetings" is activity. "Cut decision time on design reviews from 7 days to 3" is a result.
- **No baseline.** "Improve quality by 20%" is not a goal until you say 20% of what.
- **Team goals dumped on one person.** If the outcome depends on five people, write a team goal and individual contributing goals.
- **Ignoring safety and quality pressure.** A speed target with no quality measure can create the wrong behaviour.
- **Setting too many.** Three goals for a person, or five for a team, is plenty.

## FAQ: engineering SMART goals
### What is an example of a SMART goal for an engineer?
"Increase automated test coverage on the service I own from 55% to 70% by 31 July, by adding tests in two focused sessions a week." It names the area, the baseline, the target, the date and the weekly practice.

### How do I write SMART goals for an engineering team?
Start with one or two team outcomes (delivery, quality or reliability), add a baseline, then agree the practice that will move the number. Give each person one contributing goal they can influence, and review the figures in a meeting you already have.

### How many goals should an engineer have?
Two or three is a good range: one delivery or quality goal, one skill goal, and optionally one collaboration goal. More than that usually means nothing gets consistent attention.

### How often should engineering goals be reviewed?
A weekly look at the practice (did the sessions happen?) and a monthly or per-sprint look at the result works well. Change the target if the work or the system has changed.
