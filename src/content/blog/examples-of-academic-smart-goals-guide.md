Academic work rewards clear targets, but many students only set them in their head: "do well this term," "catch up," "get the dissertation done." Those hopes are understandable, and they are not yet goals. **Academic SMART goals** turn the term into a few measurable pieces of work with dates, so you can see whether a week counted.

This guide gives examples for exams, coursework, research, reading and study habits, and a method you can reuse each term. ## What makes an academic goal SMART
A useful academic goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. In practice:

- it names the module, paper, skill or output,
- it uses a number you can check (pages, papers, practice questions, words, sessions),
- it fits around lectures, work and rest,
- it connects to a real date, such as a deadline or an exam,
- it includes a way to review the week, not only the final mark.

A grade can be part of the destination. The weekly work is what you manage, because the grade is not fully in your control on any given day. "Raise my mark from 54% to 65%" needs a practice goal underneath it, or it is only a wish.

Use this sentence: *I will [produce or practise] [quantity] by/for [date], so that [exam, deadline or skill], and I'll track it by [log or scores].* The [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide has more templates.

## Academic SMART goals examples: exams and skills
1. Complete two past-paper sections in my weakest topic every week for six weeks, score each one, and review mistakes every Sunday, aiming to raise my mock mark from 58% to 70% by the exam on 15 May.
2. Memorise 40 key terms in this module over four weeks, by testing myself on 10 terms a day and doing a full recap every Sunday.
3. Attend every seminar this term and speak at least once in 8 out of 10 sessions, with one prepared comment or question each time.
4. Work through one problem set, start to finish and unchecked, twice a week for eight weeks, then mark it against the solutions.
5. Book two revision days in the calendar at least three weeks before the exam, and finish a full timed paper on each of those days.

## Academic SMART goals examples: coursework and writing
6. Write 400 words of my essay on four weekdays for five weeks, so a complete draft is ready one week before the deadline.
7. Finish the reading list for this assignment (eight chapters or papers) by 20 March, at a rate of two items a week, with a half-page note after each.
8. Get feedback on a draft from a tutor or peer at least 10 days before the deadline, by sending the draft on an agreed date.
9. Cut my usual last-minute editing panic by building a two-day buffer: complete the assignment two days early, then edit on the last day, for each of the next three submissions.
10. Use a simple structure checklist (question answered, evidence, referencing, word count) before I submit, for every assignment this term, and keep a copy of the checked list.

## Academic SMART goals examples: research and dissertations
11. Read and annotate eight papers a week for six weeks, and add each one to a summary table with methods, findings and relevance.
12. Write 500 words of new dissertation draft on five days a week for 12 weeks, reviewing the weekly total every Friday.
13. Submit my ethics or proposal paperwork by the official date, with a complete document checklist finished one week earlier.
14. Complete 30 of 50 interviews or survey responses by 30 June, checking the count every Friday and booking the next sessions a fortnight ahead.
15. Meet my supervisor every two weeks this term, with a one-page progress note and two questions sent 24 hours in advance.

## Academic SMART goals examples: reading and study habits
16. Read 20 pages of set reading on five days a week for the rest of the term, and write three bullet points after each session.
17. Study in two 45-minute blocks, four weekdays a week, for the next eight weeks, with my phone in another room.
18. Use the library or a quiet space for at least six hours a week for 10 weeks, booked in the calendar every Sunday.
19. Join or form a study group that meets weekly for an hour, for eight weeks, with a short agenda each time.
20. Sleep at least seven hours on five nights a week during the teaching term, because late-night "catch up" has been hurting the next day's lectures.

Habits like these look modest, and they often change a term more than a dramatic new timetable that lasts nine days. If the plan only works in a perfect week, shrink it. ## How to set academic goals for a term
1. **Pick one primary academic goal**, plus one habit if you have the room.
2. **Work backwards from the real dates** (exams, submissions, lab time).
3. **Write the weekly output**, not only the final grade.
4. **Put the first sessions in the calendar this week.**
5. **Review on a fixed day.** Ten minutes is enough: what happened, what the numbers say, what changes.

If two modules both feel urgent, choose the one with the nearest date or the weakest current mark, and give the other a smaller holding habit (one short session a week) until the first cycle is stable. Trying to "catch up everything" in the same week is how academic goals become a timetable you can't keep. One focused target, fully practised, will usually raise the term more than four half-started ones.

## Common mistakes with academic goals
- **Only writing the grade.** "Get a first" is a destination. Add the weekly practice that might get you there.
- **Planning a heroic timetable in week one.** An average week, including tired days, is the right design.
- **Starting too late.** A six-week practice goal cannot begin in week five.
- **Too many modules at once.** Raise the weakest one, then the next.
- **Skipping the review.** A missed week that nobody names becomes a missed month.

## FAQ: academic SMART goals
### What is an example of an academic SMART goal?
"Complete two past-paper sections in my weakest topic every week for six weeks, score each one, and review mistakes on Sunday, so I can raise my mock mark from 58% to 70% by 15 May." It names the work, the amount, the review habit and the date.

### How many academic goals should I set in a term?
One primary goal is enough for most students, plus one study habit if there is room. When that cycle is working, add another module. A short list you keep will beat a long list you ignore.

### How do I set SMART goals for a dissertation?
Set an outcome date for each chapter, then a weekly output (words, papers, or data tasks) and a regular supervisor meeting. Review the numbers every week, and put buffer time into the timeline for ethics delays and revision. The research SMART goals guide has a fuller plan.

### What if my grades aren't moving yet?
Look at the practice numbers first. If the sessions aren't happening, shrink them. If they are happening and the scores are flat, change the method (feedback, different practice, weaker topics first) before you change the ambition to zero.
