The fastest way to abandon a goal is to set one you cannot reach. "Run a marathon next month," "save a year's salary in three months" and "learn a language this weekend" sound ambitious, but they usually end in guilt, not results. **Realistic goal setting** is the skill of choosing a target that stretches you without breaking you, then writing it so you can actually follow through.

This guide shows you how to test whether a goal is realistic, how to shrink an unrealistic one without giving up, and 15 **realistic goals examples** you can adapt. You'll also see how "realistic" fits next to "achievable" in the SMART framework, and how to review a goal when life gets in the way.

## What realistic goal setting actually means
A realistic goal is one you can reasonably reach with the time, skills, money, energy and support you have *now*, or can get in time. It is not the same as an easy goal. Easy goals don't change anything. Realistic goals are slightly uncomfortable, but the path to them is visible.

Three questions separate a realistic goal from a wish:

1. **Do I have a baseline?** If you don't know where you're starting, you can't judge the size of the jump.
2. **Do I have the inputs?** Hours in the week, money, tools, skills, health, and other people's help.
3. **Is the deadline honest?** A good target with an impossible date is still unrealistic.

## Realistic vs achievable: what's the difference?
People often use the two words as if they were identical, and in everyday speech they almost are. In goal setting they tilt slightly differently:

- **Achievable** asks: *Is this possible at all for someone in my situation?*
- **Realistic** asks: *Is this possible for me, in this season of life, with everything else I already have to do?*

A goal can be achievable in theory and still unrealistic this year. Completing a degree is achievable for many people; completing it in 12 months while working two jobs and caring for a parent may not be realistic. The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) guide focuses on possibility. This article focuses on fitting the goal to your actual week.

In the SMART acronym, some versions use "realistic" for the letter R and others use "relevant". Both questions are worth asking. The [realistic in SMART](/articles/realistic-in-smart) page explains the history, and [relevant goals](/articles/relevant-goals) covers the other meaning of R.

## How to tell if a goal is realistic (a 10-minute test)
Use this checklist on any goal before you commit to it.

**1. Write the goal as a sentence with a number and a date.** "Get fitter" cannot be tested. "Walk 30 minutes, five days a week, for eight weeks" can.

**2. Find the baseline.** Track one normal week. If you currently walk once a week, five days is a stretch. Seven days, plus a gym programme, plus a diet overhaul is a leap.

**3. Count the hours.** Break the goal into weekly work. If it needs eight hours and you have two, it is not realistic yet. Cut the scope or extend the date.

**4. List the constraints.** Money, travel, childcare, health, shift patterns, exam season. A goal that ignores these will fail, even if it looks neat on paper.

**5. Check for competing goals.** Three big goals at once are often less realistic than one. See [how to prioritize goals](/articles/how-to-prioritize-goals) if everything feels urgent.

**6. Ask a reality-check question:** "If I had to bet money on this, would I?" If the honest answer is no, shrink it.

**7. Add a review date.** A goal that can't be adjusted isn't realistic, because life changes. Put a check-in on the calendar now.

## How to make an unrealistic goal realistic
You rarely need to abandon the dream. You need to change one of three things: the target, the deadline, or the plan.

- **Lower the number.** "Save 10,000 in six months" becomes "save 4,000 in six months" or "save 10,000 in 15 months."
- **Split it into stages.** A book becomes "finish a 2,000-word outline in four weeks," then a chapter, then a draft. The [break big goals into small steps](/articles/break-big-goals-into-small-steps) guide shows how.
- **Change the type of goal.** Outcome goals ("lose 15 kg") depend on many things. Process goals ("walk 30 minutes, five days a week") are easier to keep realistic, and they usually drive the outcome anyway.
- **Remove a competing commitment.** Sometimes the goal is fine, but only if something else is paused.
- **Get a missing input first.** If you need a skill, make the first goal "complete the beginners' course by 31 March," not "land the new job by 31 March."

Write the new version down. A goal you've silently downgraded in your head, but not on paper, still feels like failure.

## 15 realistic goals examples you can adapt
Adjust the numbers to your baseline. These are starting points, not rules.

### Health and habits
1. Walk 20 minutes after lunch on three workdays each week for the next six weeks, and tick each walk off in a planner.
2. Go to bed by 11 pm on weeknights for 21 days, tracked each morning.
3. Cook dinner at home four nights a week this month, instead of starting with "never get a takeaway again."
4. Drink a bottle of water by mid-afternoon every weekday for four weeks.

### Money
5. Save 10% of each paycheck for the next six months, moved automatically on payday.
6. Pay an extra 50 a month toward a debt for 12 months, reviewed quarterly.
7. Track every expense for 30 days before setting a bigger savings target, so the number is based on facts.

### Work and skills
8. Complete one online module a week for eight weeks, rather than "finish the whole certification this month."
9. Block 45 minutes of focused work before checking email, four mornings a week, for a month.
10. Ask for feedback from my manager once a month for the next quarter, with one action written down afterwards.

### Learning and personal life
11. Read 10 pages a day for 60 days, which is more realistic than "read 50 books this year" if you currently read almost nothing.
12. Practise a language for 15 minutes on five days a week for three months.
13. Message one friend or family member every Sunday for the next two months.
14. Declutter one drawer or shelf each weekend for six weeks, not the whole house in a day.
15. Take one full day off work and chores each month for the next six months, booked in the calendar in advance.

## A simple weekly method that keeps goals realistic
Realistic goal setting is not a one-time decision. It's a habit of checking the plan against real life.

- **Sunday, 15 minutes.** Look at last week's numbers. Hit the target? Keep it or raise it slightly. Missed it? Shrink the action, don't shame yourself.
- **Pick one main goal.** Two at most if one is a small habit.
- **Define a "minimum version."** If the full workout isn't possible, a 10-minute walk still counts. This keeps the habit alive on bad days.
- **Track in one place.** A notebook, a spreadsheet or an app is fine, as long as you use the same one. The [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guide has simple setups.

## Common mistakes in realistic goal setting
- **Copying someone else's target.** Their hours, money and starting point are not yours.
- **Using a best-case week as the plan.** Plan for an average week, including tired days.
- **Setting the goal in January and never reviewing it.** What was realistic in January may not be in June.
- **Confusing realistic with pessimistic.** Cutting a goal in half "just in case" can also drain motivation. Aim for a target you'd be proud to hit, and that you can defend with numbers.
- **Ignoring recovery.** Sleep, rest days and holidays are part of a realistic plan, not a failure of discipline.

## FAQ: realistic goal setting
### How do I know a goal is realistic?
Check your baseline, count the hours the goal needs each week, list your constraints and ask whether you would bet on it. If the hours don't fit, or you have no way to get a required skill or resource in time, the goal isn't realistic yet. Change the number, the date or the plan.

### What is an example of a realistic goal?
"Walk 20 minutes after lunch on three workdays each week for the next six weeks, and log each walk" is realistic for most beginners. It has a clear action, a number, a date and a weekly workload that fits a normal schedule.

### What's the difference between realistic and achievable goals?
Achievable means the goal is possible in principle. Realistic means it is possible *for you, now*, given your time, energy and other commitments. A goal can be achievable and still unrealistic this month.

### Should I lower my goals if I keep missing them?
Yes, at least temporarily. A smaller goal you keep is more useful than a big goal you abandon. After a few successful weeks you can raise the target. That's realistic goal setting, not giving up.
