A goal can be specific, measurable and dated, and still be the wrong goal. That is what the R in SMART is for. **Relevant** asks whether the goal matters to you, or to your team, right now, and whether it fits the bigger plan. If the answer is no, you will usually drop it when the week gets busy, no matter how neat the sentence looks.

This guide gives you a clear **relevant goal example**, a short test you can run on any goal, and before-and-after rewrites. It sits beside the [relevant goals](/articles/relevant-goals) guide, which covers the idea in more depth, and the [SMART goal abbreviation](/articles/smart-goal-abbreviation) page, which explains why some people use "realistic" for the same letter.

## What "relevant" means in a SMART goal
A relevant goal is connected to something you care about, or to a responsibility you actually have. It is not a goal you copied because it sounded impressive, and it is not a leftover from a version of your life that no longer exists.

Three questions help:

1. **Why does this matter now?** If you can't finish the sentence, the goal isn't relevant yet.
2. **What bigger plan does it serve?** A course, a health need, a team target, a family commitment, a money problem.
3. **If I succeeded, would my week actually be better?** If you wouldn't notice, it may be a distraction.

Relevant is different from achievable. Achievable asks "can I do this?" Relevant asks "should I do this, now?" A goal can be possible and still be a poor use of your next eight weeks. The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) and [realistic goal setting](/articles/realistic-goal-setting) guides cover the "can I?" side.

## A relevant goal example, built step by step
Imagine someone writes: "I will complete an advanced photography course by 30 September, studying four hours a week."

On the surface, this is already quite SMART. It is specific, measurable, probably achievable and time-bound. But relevance depends on context.

**If it is relevant:** they have a trip in October, they want to take better photos for a small business, or photography is the one hobby that reliably restores their energy. The reason is clear.

**If it is not relevant:** they signed up because a friend did, they already have three unfinished courses, and the real problem in their week is sleep and a work deadline. The course is a detour.

A more relevant rewrite, in the second case, might be: "I will be in bed by 11 pm on weeknights for the next four weeks, and complete the current work project by 15 April, because those two things are what my weeks actually need." The photography course can wait until the next cycle.

The lesson is not that hobbies are a waste. It is that relevance is about timing and fit, not about whether a goal is "worthy" in the abstract.

## More relevant goal examples
**Work.** "Reduce the time I spend writing the weekly report from three hours to 90 minutes by 31 May, by building a template and using last week's numbers as a starting point, so I can spend Monday morning on client work instead." The relevance is explicit: more time for the work that matters.

**Health.** "Walk for 30 minutes after dinner on five days a week for eight weeks, so I have more energy in the evenings with my family." The family evenings are the reason, not an abstract idea of fitness.

**Money.** "Move 150 into an emergency account on payday for the next six months, because an unexpected bill last quarter put me into overdraft." The reason is a real event, not a general wish to "be better with money."

**Learning.** "Complete the data module by 30 June, studying two evenings a week, because the next project in July will need it." The project date makes the learning relevant, not just interesting.

**Team.** "Agree a written handover checklist with the team by 30 April and use it on every shift for three months, because two recent incidents started with missed information." The incidents are the relevance test.

## A 5-question test for relevance
Use this on any goal before you commit time to it:

1. **Can I name the reason in one sentence, without jargon?**
2. **Is this the best use of the next 4 to 8 weeks, given what else is on my plate?**
3. **Does it support a plan I already have, or is it a new plan I don't have room for?**
4. **Would I still want this on a tired week, not only on a motivated morning?**
5. **If I dropped it, would I regret it in three months?**

If you answer no to three or more, rewrite or postpone the goal. Postponing on purpose is a relevant choice. Silently keeping a goal you don't care about is how lists fill up and weeks stay the same.

## How to rewrite a goal that isn't relevant
- **Connect it to a real reason.** Add "so that…" and make the reason specific.
- **Change the goal, not just the wording.** If the reason points to sleep, write a sleep goal. Don't force the original topic to fit.
- **Shorten the timeframe.** A four-week trial can test whether the goal still matters once the novelty fades.
- **Drop it on purpose.** Write "not now" and a date to look again. That is cleaner than leaving it on a list.

Then put a first action in the calendar only for the goal that passed the test. The [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) formula will help you write the new sentence, and the [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guide will help you review it.

Relevance also changes when your role or season changes. A study goal that was relevant before exams can become less so once the exam is over, and a work goal can stop being relevant after a project ships. That is not a reason to feel inconsistent. It is a reason to put a review date on the goal from the start, so you can retire it cleanly and choose the next relevant one, instead of dragging last season's target into a week that needs something else.

## Common mistakes with the R in SMART
- **Writing a reason that could apply to any goal.** "To be a better person" or "for success" is not a test. Be specific.
- **Confusing relevant with urgent.** A noisy task can feel urgent and still be a poor goal.
- **Keeping last year's goals out of habit.** Relevance expires. Review it at the start of each cycle.
- **Letting someone else's priority become yours without agreement.** A manager's goal can be relevant to your role, but only if you understand how your work connects to it.
- **Using relevant as an excuse to avoid hard goals.** A difficult goal can still be relevant. Don't drop something just because it is uncomfortable.

## FAQ: relevant goal examples
### What is a relevant goal example?
"Walk for 30 minutes after dinner on five days a week for eight weeks, so I have more energy in the evenings with my family" is a relevant goal. The action is clear, and the reason is a real part of the person's life, not a slogan.

### How do I know if a goal is relevant?
Ask why it matters now, what plan it serves, and whether success would improve your actual week. If you can't answer those questions honestly, the goal isn't relevant yet. Rewrite it or postpone it.

### Is the R in SMART relevant or realistic?
Both words are used. Relevant asks whether the goal matters and fits your plans. Realistic asks whether you can do it with your current time and resources. Ask both. This article focuses on relevance; realistic goal setting covers the other meaning.

### Can a goal be relevant and still be the wrong size?
Yes. A relevant goal can still be too big or too vague. Fix relevance first, then check the other SMART letters: make it measurable, achievable and time-bound.
