Research projects slip for a familiar reason: the work is large, the finish line is far away, and day-to-day progress is hard to see. "Work on my dissertation" or "get the study done" can fill a calendar and still leave you unsure whether the week counted. **Research SMART goals** break that work into specific, measurable pieces with dates, so you always know what "on track" looks like.

This guide is for students, academic researchers and people doing research inside a company or public organisation. The examples are about planning and output, not about research methods. Always follow your institution's ethics, data and supervision rules.

## Why research needs SMART goals
Research has long stretches of reading, thinking and revision that don't look like progress. SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) help because they:

- **Turn a big project into weekly output.** Pages drafted, papers read, interviews completed, figures prepared.
- **Make supervision easier.** A clear goal is easier to discuss than "I'm still working on it."
- **Protect the calendar.** Teaching, lab work, a job or family will fill every unclaimed hour.
- **Show risk early.** If you miss two weekly writing targets in a row, you still have time to change the plan.

For the sentence-level formula, use the [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide. If your research is for a course or thesis, the [SMART goals for studying](/articles/smart-goals-for-studying) article covers the study habits that sit around the project.

## How to write a SMART goal for a research project
Use this structure: *I will [produce or complete] [quantity] of [output] by [date], so that [the next stage can start], and I'll review it on [checkpoint].*

Choose outputs you can count: words or pages drafted, papers read and annotated, dataset records cleaned, interviews or experiments completed, figures or tables prepared, sections sent to a supervisor, or ethics and registration tasks submitted.

Process goals ("write 400 words, five days a week") often work better than outcome goals ("finish the thesis by June") alone. You still need the outcome as a destination, but the weekly number is what you manage. For ways to put numbers on work that feels fuzzy, see the [measurable goals guide](/articles/measurable-goals-guide) and [measurable objectives examples in research](/articles/measurable-objectives-examples-in-research).

## Research SMART goals: literature review and reading
1. Read and annotate 8 papers a week for the next 6 weeks, and add each one to a summary table with methods, findings and relevance.
2. Finish a 2,000-word literature review draft by 30 March, writing 400 words on four days a week, and send it to my supervisor by 2 April.
3. Build a reference library of 40 sources by 15 April, using the same citation tool and a consistent tag for each theme.
4. Update my notes on one key debate every Friday for 8 weeks, so the review stays organised as I read.

## Research SMART goals: writing a paper, thesis or dissertation
5. Write 500 words of new draft material on five days a week for 12 weeks, tracking a weekly total rather than a perfect daily streak.
6. Complete a full first draft of chapter 2 (about 6,000 words) by 31 May, with an outline done by 15 April and a mid-point review on 15 May.
7. Revise one chapter a week for the next 4 weeks, using a written checklist (argument, evidence, references, clarity) before I send it on.
8. Submit a conference abstract of 250–300 words by the official deadline, after two reviews from a colleague, booked into my calendar 10 days before.

A writing goal should include a "minimum version" for difficult weeks: even 200 words keeps the project warm. The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) guide is useful if your first target was too ambitious.

## Research SMART goals: data, methods and analysis
9. Complete ethics or approval paperwork and submit it by 20 March, with a checklist of documents finished one week earlier.
10. Pilot my survey or interview guide with 3 people by 15 April, and finalise the instrument within 7 days of the last pilot.
11. Collect 30 of 50 interviews by 30 June, booking at least 4 sessions every fortnight and reviewing the count every Friday.
12. Clean the core dataset (missing values, codes, variable names) by 15 May, working in 90-minute blocks four days a week, and keep a log of decisions.
13. Produce the first set of descriptive tables or figures by 31 May, and discuss them with my supervisor in the following week.

## Research SMART goals: teamwork and project management
Research is often a group effort. Shared goals prevent the project from living in one person's head.

14. Hold a 30-minute research meeting every two weeks for the next six months, with a written agenda and a short note of decisions afterwards.
15. Agree who owns each work package by 15 March, with a one-page timeline and a named deputy for each part.
16. Share a written progress note (half a page, plus current risks) on the first Monday of every month until the report is submitted.
17. Complete the shared data documentation by 30 June, so any team member can rerun the main analysis from the files.

## A simple planning rhythm for research
- **Term or quarter:** set one outcome goal (a chapter, a data milestone, a submission).
- **Month:** set one or two output goals (words, interviews, figures).
- **Week:** choose the process target (writing days, reading quota, lab sessions).
- **Friday, 15 minutes:** compare the number with the plan. Ahead, on track, or behind? Adjust next week, not next term.

This is the same idea as [breaking big goals into small steps](/articles/break-big-goals-into-small-steps), applied to a project that may last a year or more. Keep the plan in one place and review it on a fixed day. The [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guide has simple formats that work for research as well as personal goals.

## A sample 12-week research plan
Here is how the pieces can fit together for a student or a small team working toward a draft or a report:

- **Weeks 1–2:** refine the question, finish the search strategy and set up the reference library. Goal: 20 key sources tagged.
- **Weeks 3–5:** literature notes and a draft review section. Goal: 8 papers a week and a 2,000-word draft.
- **Weeks 6–8:** data collection or analysis, with a weekly count (interviews, records or figures).
- **Weeks 9–11:** write the results and discussion in 500-word sessions, five days a week.
- **Week 12:** revise with a checklist and send the draft for comment, leaving a few days of buffer.

You would not run every goal at full intensity in the same week. The active goal changes as the project moves. That is the point of reviewing on a fixed day: you retire a completed milestone and promote the next one, instead of carrying 10 goals at once.

## Common mistakes with research goals
- **Only setting the end date.** "Submit in September" is a deadline, not a plan.
- **Counting hours instead of output.** Eight hours of "working on it" can mean eight hours of rereading notes. Count pages, papers or tasks completed.
- **No buffer.** Ethics delays, access problems and illness are normal. Build two or three weeks of slack into the timeline.
- **Waiting for a perfect block of time.** Regular short sessions beat an occasional perfect day.
- **Hiding from supervision.** A missed target is useful information. Bring it to the next meeting while there is still time to change the plan.

## FAQ: research SMART goals
### What is an example of a SMART goal for research?
"Write 500 words of new draft material on five days a week for 12 weeks, and review the weekly total every Friday, so the first draft of chapter 2 is done by 31 May." It names the output, the amount, the schedule and the date.

### How do I set SMART goals for a dissertation?
Set an outcome date for each chapter, then a monthly output (words or sections) and a weekly process target (writing days). Review the numbers with your supervisor at each meeting, and adjust early if a chapter is slipping.

### How many research goals should I have at once?
One primary writing or analysis goal, plus one smaller process goal (such as reading or data collection), is enough for most weeks. More than that usually means nothing gets a full session.

### What if my research depends on other people?
Put their part in the timeline as a risk, not as something you pretend to control. Set goals around what you can prepare, follow up and do in the meantime (recruitment messages, a backup sample, analysis of data you already have).
