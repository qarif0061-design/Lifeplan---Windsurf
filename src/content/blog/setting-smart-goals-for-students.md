Students are often told to "set goals" at the start of a term and then left with a blank page. The usual results are slogans ("work harder," "get better grades") that are hard to act on and impossible to review. **Setting SMART goals for students** means turning those slogans into a small number of clear, measurable targets with dates, then checking them every week.

This is a how-to, not only a list. You'll find a simple method, examples for study, habits and life outside class, and notes for parents and teachers who are helping. ## Why SMART works well for students
School already has dates: assignments, exams, terms. What it often lacks is a bridge between "I want a better grade" and "what do I do on Tuesday evening?" SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound) builds that bridge.

- **Specific** names the subject, the skill or the habit.
- **Measurable** uses marks, pages, practice papers, or sessions completed.
- **Achievable** fits the goal around classes, work, rest and family, not around a perfect week.
- **Relevant** connects the goal to an exam, a course requirement, or something the student actually cares about.
- **Time-bound** uses a real date: a test, a submission, or a four- to eight-week cycle.

A weak goal is "do better in maths." A strong one is "complete two past-paper sections on algebra every week for six weeks, score each one, and review mistakes on Sunday, so I can raise my mock mark from 54% to 65% by 15 March."

## How to set a SMART goal with a student (5 steps)
**1. Start with the problem or the wish, in their words.** "I'm behind in chemistry." "I want to make the team." Don't polish it yet.

**2. Find a baseline.** What is the current mark, the current habit, the current weekly practice? Without this, the target is a guess.

**3. Write one sentence with a number and a date.** Use: *I will [action] [how much or how often] by [date], so that [reason], and I'll track it by [method].* The [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide has more templates.

**4. Check it will fit a normal week.** Count the hours. If the plan only works when every evening is free, it isn't achievable. Shrink it. See [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) and [realistic goal setting](/articles/realistic-goal-setting).

**5. Add a weekly review and a first action.** Put the first study session or habit in the calendar this week, and pick a 10-minute review day, often Sunday.

Students should write the final sentence themselves. Goals they have worded are more likely to be kept than goals handed to them.

## SMART goal examples for studying
1. Finish two chemistry past-paper sections every Tuesday and Thursday for six weeks, and score each one, aiming to raise my mock average from 58% to 70% by the exam.
2. Write 300 words of my coursework on four weekdays for the next five weeks, so a full draft is ready one week before the deadline.
3. Read 15 pages of the set novel on five days a week for four weeks, and write three bullet points after each chapter.
4. Spend 25 minutes on vocabulary in my language class, five days a week for eight weeks, and test myself every Sunday.
5. Meet a teacher or tutor once a week for four weeks with two questions prepared, and act on one piece of feedback each time.

## SMART goal examples beyond the classroom
School goals matter, but they're not the only ones students need.

6. Practise my instrument or sport for 20 minutes on four days a week for eight weeks, and keep a simple log.
7. Be off my phone by 10:30 pm on school nights for the next month, charging it outside the bedroom.
8. Pack my bag and set out tomorrow's clothes before 9 pm on school nights for three weeks.
9. Save a fixed amount from each bit of work or allowance for 10 weeks, in a separate place, toward a specific purchase.
10. Message or meet one friend every weekend for two months, booked at least two days ahead, if I want a stronger social habit after a lonely term.

## How many goals should a student have?
One academic goal and, optionally, one habit goal is enough for most terms. Three or four at once usually means none of them get a full session. When a goal is hit, add another. That rhythm teaches follow-through better than a long January list.

## Advice for parents and teachers
- **Ask questions rather than writing the goal for them.** "How will you know it's working?" is better than "you should do two papers a week."
- **Praise the practice, not only the grade.** Grades move slowly. Weekly sessions are what the student controls.
- **Help with the calendar, not just the ambition.** A goal with no slot in the week is a wish.
- **Review together, briefly.** Ten minutes on Sunday is better than a long speech after a bad result.
- **Allow a rewrite.** If the target was too big, shrinking it is a skill, not a failure.

## How to review a student goal
- **Weekly:** Did the sessions happen? If not, was the plan too big?
- **After two or three weeks:** Is the measure moving (scores, pages, practice days)?
- **At the end of the cycle:** Keep the habit, raise the target, or choose the next subject.

## A simple weekly plan students can copy
Once the SMART sentence is written, the week still needs a shape. A pattern that works for many students is:

- **Two or three midweek sessions** of 25 to 45 minutes, on named days, for the main academic goal.
- **One short Sunday review:** what got done, what the scores or pages look like, and what changes next week.
- **One buffer slot** at the weekend, used only if a weekday session was missed.

This is more reliable than "I'll study when I have time." It also makes it obvious, after two weeks, whether the goal was too big. If both midweek sessions keep slipping, shrink the session before you shrink the ambition to zero. A smaller plan that happens will beat a heroic plan that never starts.

## Common mistakes when setting student goals
- **Goals about grades only, with no weekly action.** A grade is an outcome. The practice is the goal you manage.
- **Copying a classmate's target.** Their starting point is not yours.
- **Starting in exam week.** Set the cycle early enough that there is time to adjust.
- **Too many subjects at once.** Raise one, then the next.
- **Adults taking over the wording.** Ownership matters.

## FAQ: setting SMART goals for students
### How do you set SMART goals for students?
Start with the student's own wish, find a baseline, write one sentence with a number and a date, check that it fits a normal week, and add a first action plus a weekly review. The student should write the final sentence.

### What is an example of a SMART goal for a student?
"Complete two chemistry past-paper sections every Tuesday and Thursday for six weeks, score each one, and review mistakes on Sunday, so I can raise my mock mark from 54% to 65% by 15 March."

### How many goals should a student set at a time?
One main academic goal is enough, plus one small habit if there is room. Finish or review that cycle before adding more.

### How can parents help without taking over?
Ask questions, help find time in the calendar, praise the weekly practice, and keep the review short. Let the student own the wording and the first rewrite if the target was too big.
