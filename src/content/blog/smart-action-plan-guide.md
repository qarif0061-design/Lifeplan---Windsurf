A SMART goal tells you where you're going. It does not, on its own, tell you what to do on Tuesday. That is the job of a **SMART action plan**: a short, dated list of steps that moves the goal from a sentence in a document to work in your week. Without it, even a well-written goal tends to sit unused.

This guide shows you how to build a SMART action plan, gives you a template you can copy, and walks through a full example. If you already have the goal and only need the sentence formula, keep the [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide nearby. For the official pillar page on this topic, see [SMART action plan](/articles/smart-action-plan).

## What a SMART action plan is (and isn't)
A SMART action plan is the working document that sits under a Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound goal. It answers four practical questions:

- What is the first step?
- What comes after that, in order?
- When will each step happen?
- How will I know a step is done?

It is not a 20-page project plan, and it is not a vague list of hopes. Three to eight steps is enough for most personal and team goals. If you need more than that, the goal is probably still too big and should be split.

An action plan is also different from a to-do list. A to-do list collects tasks. An action plan connects those tasks to one goal, a date and a way to review progress.

## Why goals stall without a plan
People often stop at the SMART sentence because it already feels like work. Then a week passes, then a month, and the goal is still a sentence. Three things usually went missing:

1. **No first action.** "Save 3,000 this year" does not tell you what to do on payday.
2. **No weekly slot.** If the work isn't in the calendar, other work will fill the time.
3. **No review.** Without a check-in, a missed week becomes a missed month before anyone notices.

The [SMART goal steps](/articles/smart-goal-steps) and [SMART goal approach](/articles/smart-goal-approach) guides cover how to write the goal. This article starts at the moment after that, when you turn the goal into steps. The [break big goals into small steps](/articles/break-big-goals-into-small-steps) page is a close companion.

## How to write a SMART action plan in 6 steps
**1. Write the goal in one sentence.** If it has no number and no date, fix that first. A plan can't rescue a vague goal.

**2. List the work backwards from the deadline.** What must be true two weeks before the date? One month before? This stops you starting in the wrong place.

**3. Turn each chunk into a step you can finish.** "Work on the report" is not a step. "Draft the first two sections by Friday" is.

**4. Give every step a date and an owner.** If you're working alone, you are the owner. If you're in a team, name the person.

**5. Add a first action for this week.** The plan is not finished until something is in the calendar in the next seven days.

**6. Set a review date.** Weekly for short goals, fortnightly for longer ones. Ten minutes is enough. The [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guide has simple ways to run that review.

## A simple SMART action plan template
Copy this and fill it in:

- **Goal (SMART sentence):**
- **Deadline:**
- **Success measure:** (the number you'll check)
- **Steps:**
  1. Action — date — done when…
  2. Action — date — done when…
  3. Action — date — done when…
- **First action this week:**
- **Review day:**
- **Minimum version:** (what you'll do if the week goes wrong)

The minimum version matters. If the full step isn't possible, a smaller version keeps the plan alive instead of breaking the streak.

## Worked example: a savings goal
**Goal:** "I will move 250 into a separate savings account on the first day of every month for 12 months, reaching 3,000 by 31 December, because I want a buffer for unexpected bills."

**Action plan:**

1. Open a named savings account this week, and turn on a reminder for the 1st of each month. Done when the account exists and the reminder is set.
2. On 1 January (or the next payday), make the first transfer of 250. Done when the receipt is saved.
3. Each month, on the 1st, transfer 250 before other spending. Done when the balance has increased by 250.
4. On the first Sunday of each quarter, review the balance and any missed months. Done when the number is written down and the next quarter's transfers are still in the calendar.
5. In December, check the total against 3,000 and decide whether to keep the habit in the new year.

**First action this week:** open the account and set the reminder, today or tomorrow.

**Review day:** the first Sunday of each month, 10 minutes.

**Minimum version:** if a month is tight, transfer 100 rather than skip the habit.

That is a complete SMART action plan. It is short, dated and easy to check.

## Worked example: a work or study goal
**Goal:** "I will complete an online course in my field by 30 June, finishing one module a week, so I can use the skill on the next project."

**Action plan:**

1. Choose the course and block a 45-minute study slot on four weekdays, this week. Done when the slots are in the calendar.
2. Complete module 1 by the end of week 1, and write three bullet points on how I'll use it.
3. Complete one module a week for the next 10 weeks, with a catch-up slot on Saturday if a weekday is missed.
4. Build a small practice task after module 6 (a short project, a checklist, or a worked example).
5. Finish the assessment or final project by 20 June, leaving 10 days of buffer before the deadline.

**First action this week:** enrol and complete the first lesson.

The same pattern works for a report, a launch or a training plan: outcome, weekly output, first step, review.

## How to keep the plan moving
- **Look at it on a fixed day.** A plan you don't open is not a plan.
- **Update the next step, not the whole document.** When a step is done, write the next one if it wasn't already there.
- **Change the plan when the week tells you to.** If you miss two reviews in a row, the steps are too big. Shrink them. That is planning, not failure.
- **Keep it in one place.** A page in a notebook, a simple doc or an app all work, as long as you use the same one.

## Common mistakes with action plans
- **Too many steps on day one.** You don't need the whole year mapped. You need the next two or three steps and a review habit.
- **Steps with no "done when".** If you can't tell whether a step is finished, it will hang open for weeks.
- **No time in the calendar.** A date on a list is not the same as a blocked hour.
- **Planning instead of starting.** If you've been writing the plan for longer than you've been doing the first step, stop and do the step.
- **No link back to the measure.** The plan should move the number in the goal. If the steps don't change the number, rewrite the steps.

## FAQ: SMART action plans
### What is a SMART action plan?
It is a short, dated list of steps that sits under a SMART goal. Each step has an action, a date and a clear finish line, plus a first action for this week and a review day. It turns the goal into work you can put in a calendar.

### How is an action plan different from a SMART goal?
The goal is the destination (what, how much, by when). The action plan is the route (the steps you'll take, in order, with dates). You need both. A goal without a plan is a wish; a plan without a goal is a pile of tasks.

### How detailed should the plan be?
Detailed enough that the next step is obvious, and no more. Three to eight steps is plenty for most personal goals. Add the later steps as you get closer, rather than inventing a perfect plan you won't follow.

### How often should I update it?
Check it weekly. Tick off finished steps, adjust anything that slipped, and make sure the next seven days still have a first action. A monthly review is useful for longer goals, but it shouldn't replace the weekly check.
