You have written a goal. It sounds reasonable. But is it actually a good one? **SMART analysis** is the quick check that answers that question. You run the goal through five tests (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound), score it, and fix whichever part fails. Done properly it takes about two minutes, and it catches the problems that cause most goals to stall: vague wording, no number, an unrealistic target or no date.

This guide explains what a SMART analysis is, gives you a scorecard you can reuse, and walks through several **examples of SMART analysis** so you can see what a weak goal and a strong goal look like side by side.

## What is a SMART analysis?
A SMART analysis is a structured review of a goal against the SMART criteria. Instead of asking "is this a good goal?" and answering from gut feeling, you ask five specific questions and write down the answers. If any answer is "no" or "not sure", you rewrite that part.

It is different from a SWOT analysis, which looks at strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats around a situation. You can use both: SWOT helps you decide *what* to aim at, and SMART analysis checks that the goal you wrote down is *well formed*. If you want to see how they work together, read about [SMART goals and SWOT analysis](/articles/smart-goals-and-swot-analysis).

You can run a SMART analysis on:
- a goal you are about to start,
- a goal that is stuck and you don't know why,
- a goal someone else wrote, such as a team member or a student,
- a draft goal from a performance review.

## The 5-question SMART analysis scorecard
Score each question 0 (no), 1 (partly) or 2 (yes). A goal scoring 9 or 10 is ready. Anything below 8 needs a rewrite.

| Letter | Question to ask | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Specific | Could someone else read this and know exactly what I will do? | 0–2 |
| Measurable | Is there a number or clear evidence that shows progress? | 0–2 |
| Achievable | Is it a stretch from my current level, but possible with my time and resources? | 0–2 |
| Relevant | Does it connect to something that matters to me or my team right now? | 0–2 |
| Time-bound | Is there a deadline and at least one checkpoint? | 0–2 |

If you'd like a deeper definition of each letter, the [SMART criteria for goals](/articles/smart-criteria-for-goals) guide covers them one by one.

## How to run a SMART analysis step by step
**1. Write the goal exactly as it stands.** Don't polish it first. You are testing the current version.

**2. Go through the five questions.** For each one, write one sentence of evidence. "Measurable: no number." "Time-bound: deadline is 'soon'."

**3. Find the weakest letter.** That is where you rewrite first. Often fixing one letter fixes two. Adding a number often makes a goal more specific too.

**4. Rewrite as a single sentence.** Use the formula from the [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide: *I will [action] [how much or how often] by [date], because [reason].*

**5. Re-score.** If it reaches 9 or 10, add a first step and a review date.

## Example of SMART analysis 1: a fitness goal
**Original goal:** "Get fitter this year."

| Letter | Analysis | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Specific | "Fitter" can mean anything | 0 |
| Measurable | No number | 0 |
| Achievable | Unknown | 1 |
| Relevant | Probably, but no reason given | 1 |
| Time-bound | "This year" is too loose | 1 |

**Total: 3 out of 10.**

**Rewritten:** "I will walk or jog for 30 minutes, four times a week, for the next 12 weeks, and log each session, so that I can run 5 km without stopping by the end of February."

New score: 10 out of 10. It names the activity, number, timeframe and reason.

## Example of SMART analysis 2: a work goal
**Original goal:** "Improve customer service response times."

- **Specific:** Partly. It names an area, but not which channel or team. (1)
- **Measurable:** No target given. (0)
- **Achievable:** Unknown without a baseline. (1)
- **Relevant:** Yes, tied to a business need. (2)
- **Time-bound:** No date. (0)

**Total: 4 out of 10.**

**Rewritten:** "Reduce the average first reply time on email support tickets from 8 hours to 4 hours by 31 March by adding a shared inbox rota and a weekly review of slow tickets."

The fix: add the baseline, the target, the date and the method. Now it can be checked, owned and discussed in a one-to-one meeting. For more workplace examples see [SMART goals examples for work](/articles/smart-goals-examples-for-work).

## Example of SMART analysis 3: a study goal
**Original goal:** "Study harder for my exams."

- **Specific:** No. (0)
- **Measurable:** No. (0)
- **Achievable:** Not clear. (1)
- **Relevant:** Yes, exams matter. (2)
- **Time-bound:** Exam date is implied but not stated. (1)

**Total: 4 out of 10.**

**Rewritten:** "I will complete two past-paper sections in chemistry every Tuesday and Thursday for the next 6 weeks and mark each one, so I can raise my average mock score from 58% to 70% by the exam on 15 March."

## Example of SMART analysis 4: a goal that fails on "Achievable"
**Original goal:** "Save 10,000 in three months on a monthly income of 1,800."

Everything else is fine here: it's specific, measurable, relevant and time-bound. But the maths fails. Saving 3,333 a month from an income of 1,800 is not possible without drastic changes. This is why Achievable matters.

**Rewritten:** "Save 5,000 in 12 months by moving 420 into savings on the first of each month and cutting two subscriptions, reviewing progress each quarter."

The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) guide shows how to check whether a target is realistic before you commit to it.

## Common problems a SMART analysis uncovers
- **A goal with no number.** The fix is almost always to add a count, a percentage, a time or a frequency. See the [measurable goals guide](/articles/measurable-goals-guide).
- **A goal with no date.** "Soon" and "this year" are not deadlines. Use an actual date and one midpoint checkpoint. The [time-bound goals](/articles/time-bound-goals) guide has more.
- **A goal nobody cares about.** If you cannot say why it matters, you will not do it. Check that it is [relevant](/articles/relevant-goals).
- **A goal that is really three goals.** "Eat better, exercise more and sleep earlier" should be split and analysed separately.
- **A goal that describes a wish, not an action.** "Be happier" cannot be analysed until you pick a behaviour, such as "Spend 20 minutes outdoors four days a week."

## Use SMART analysis for teams and students
A SMART analysis is especially useful when you review other people's goals, because the scorecard keeps feedback objective. A manager can ask "which letter is weakest?" instead of saying "this goal isn't good enough". A teacher can give students the five questions as a self-check before handing in an action plan. It is also a gentle way to coach: the person rewrites their own goal, so they own the result.

## FAQ: SMART analysis
### What is a SMART analysis of a goal?
It is a quick review of a goal against the five SMART criteria. For each letter you ask whether the goal passes, score it, and rewrite the weak parts. The result is a goal that is clear, measurable and has a deadline.

### How is SMART analysis different from SWOT analysis?
SWOT analyses a situation (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats). SMART analysis checks whether a specific goal is well written. They complement each other: use SWOT to choose a direction, then SMART analysis to sharpen the goal.

### How long does a SMART analysis take?
About two minutes for a single goal once you know the questions. A first pass on a complicated team goal might take ten minutes, mostly spent on finding a baseline number.

### What score should a goal get?
On the 10-point scorecard above, aim for 9 or 10. A score of 7 or 8 is usable but has a weakness worth fixing. Anything below 7 should be rewritten before you start.
