People type "SMART goal abbreviation" into a search box when they want a straight answer: what do the five letters stand for, and how do I use them? The short version is that **SMART** stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. The longer version is more useful, because two of the letters are sometimes given different words, and a list of letters is not the same as a usable goal.

This guide gives you the abbreviation, the common variations, a clear example for each letter, and a way to check a goal you've already written. For a deeper walk through all five words, see [Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound](/articles/specific-measurable-achievable-relevant-time-bound). For the history of the framework, see [Doran 1981 SMART goals](/articles/doran-1981-smart-goals).

## The SMART abbreviation at a glance
| Letter | Most common word | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| S | Specific | What exactly will I do? |
| M | Measurable | How will I know I'm making progress? |
| A | Achievable (sometimes Attainable) | Can I do this with what I have? |
| R | Relevant (sometimes Realistic) | Why does this matter, and is it possible for me now? |
| T | Time-bound (sometimes Timely or Time-based) | By when, and when do I check in? |

If you remember only one thing, remember the five questions, not just the five words. The words can shift slightly between textbooks, employers and teachers. The questions stay stable.

## S is for Specific
A specific goal names the action or result clearly enough that someone else could understand it.

**Weak:** "Improve my writing."
**Strong:** "Write 500 words of my report on four weekday mornings."

If two people would describe different finish lines, the S is not done yet. Ask what, who, where and which part of the work, and keep the sentence tight.

## M is for Measurable
A measurable goal has a number, a frequency or clear evidence. Without it, you will judge progress by mood.

**Weak:** "Save more."
**Strong:** "Save 200 a month, starting from 0 in a separate account."

Hours, counts, percentages, scores and yes/no milestones all work. Pick one primary measure. The [measurable goals guide](/articles/measurable-goals-guide) has more ways to measure things that seem soft, such as confidence or communication.

## A is for Achievable (or Attainable)
This letter asks whether the goal is a stretch, not a fantasy. It should be possible with your time, skills, money and current commitments, or with skills you can get in time.

**Weak:** "Run a marathon next month," from no running at all.
**Strong:** "Run 5 km without stopping within nine weeks, following a beginner plan three times a week."

"Achievable" and "attainable" mean the same thing here. The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) guide shows how to test this honestly, using a baseline and a count of the hours the goal needs each week.

## R is for Relevant — or Realistic?
This is the letter that causes the most confusion, because two words are in common use.

- **Relevant** asks: does this goal matter to me, and does it fit my bigger plans?
- **Realistic** asks: can I actually do this in my current life?

The original 1981 paper by George Doran used *realistic*. Many modern guides use *relevant*, because "realistic" overlaps with "achievable." Both questions are worth asking. If your goals tend to be random, lean on relevant. If they tend to be too big, lean on realistic. The [relevant goals](/articles/relevant-goals) and [realistic goal setting](/articles/realistic-goal-setting) guides cover each side.

**Weak:** "Learn Japanese" with no trip, no work need and no time.
**Strong:** "Reach basic conversational Japanese by October so I can handle a work trip, by practising 15 minutes a day."

## T is for Time-bound
A time-bound goal has a deadline and, ideally, a checkpoint before that deadline.

**Weak:** "Launch my website eventually."
**Strong:** "Launch my website by 15 March, with draft pages done by 15 February."

"Soon" and "this year" are not dates. Use a real one. For longer goals, add a mid-point review. See [time-bound goals](/articles/time-bound-goals) for help choosing a timeframe that fits the work.

## Put the abbreviation into one sentence
Here is the same goal, built letter by letter.

**Rough idea:** "I want to get fitter."

- **Specific:** Walk after work.
- **Measurable:** 30 minutes, four days a week. Current baseline: one short walk at weekends.
- **Achievable:** Four days is a stretch from one, but the time exists.
- **Relevant:** I want more energy in the evenings with my family.
- **Time-bound:** Eight weeks, reviewed every Sunday.

**Finished goal:** "I will walk for 30 minutes after work on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday for the next eight weeks, and log each walk, so I have more energy in the evenings."

That is the abbreviation at work. For more finished examples, see the [SMART goals examples](/articles/smart-goals-examples) hub. If you want a slightly longer framework, [SMARTER goals](/articles/smarter-goals-meaning) adds *evaluate* and *readjust* — which is just the review habit made explicit.


## A 60-second check you can reuse
When you next write a goal, read it out and tick the letters:

- **S:** Could a colleague or friend describe the same finish line?
- **M:** Is there a number, or a clear yes/no, I can write down this week?
- **A:** Have I counted the hours, and do they fit an ordinary week?
- **R:** Can I finish the sentence "this matters because…"?
- **T:** Is there a real date, and at least one checkpoint before it?

A goal can miss one letter and still be usable, but two missing letters is usually why it stalls. Fix the weakest letter first. Adding a number often makes a goal more specific at the same time, and adding a date often forces you to check whether the target is achievable.

You can also use the abbreviation as a coaching tool. Instead of telling someone "this goal isn't good enough," ask "which letter is weakest?" People usually see the gap themselves, and they keep ownership of the rewrite.

## Common mix-ups with the SMART abbreviation
- **Treating it as a slogan.** Writing the five words in a document does not make the goal SMART. Add the number and the date.
- **Mixing up abbreviation and acronym.** In everyday use, people call SMART both. Strictly, it is an acronym (a word made of initials). Searchers often use "abbreviation," which is why this page uses that word.
- **Using only outcome words.** "Be healthier" is not yet a SMART goal, even if you can recite the letters.
- **Forgetting a baseline.** "Improve by 20%" is incomplete if you don't say 20% of what.
- **Never reviewing.** The letters help you write the goal. A weekly check helps you keep it. The [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guide covers that part.

## FAQ: SMART goal abbreviation
### What is the SMART goal abbreviation?
SMART stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. Some organisations use Attainable for A, or Realistic for R, and Time-based or Timely for T. The five questions behind the letters matter more than which synonym you use.

### Is the R in SMART relevant or realistic?
Both are used. The original paper said realistic; many modern guides say relevant. Ask both questions: can I do this, and does it matter? Either word works if you check both.

### What does the A in SMART stand for?
Usually Achievable, sometimes Attainable. Both mean the same thing: the goal is possible given your time, skills and resources, not only impressive on paper.

### Do I have to put all five letters into one sentence?
No. You can write a short goal sentence and keep the reasoning (why it's relevant, why it's achievable) in a note underneath. What matters is that every part is clear, not that the sentence is crowded.
