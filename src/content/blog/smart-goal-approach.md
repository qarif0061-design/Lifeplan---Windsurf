Most people have heard of SMART goals. Fewer have a clear way to *use* them when they're sitting in front of a blank page. The **SMART goal approach** is that method: a short sequence of steps that turns a vague wish into a goal you can act on this week. It is not a new framework. It is a practical way to apply the one you already know.

This guide walks through the approach from start to finish, works a full example in public, and shows where people usually get stuck. If you want a one-page formula for the final sentence, keep the [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide open as you go.

## What the SMART goal approach is
The SMART goal approach is a process, not a slogan. You start with a rough idea, test it against the five criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound), rewrite it as a single sentence, attach a first action and a review date, and then track it.

That is different from simply "writing a SMART goal," which often stops at a tidy sentence and never reaches the calendar. The approach includes the work *after* the sentence: breaking the goal into steps, putting the first one in your week, and coming back to check whether it's working.

For a wider look at building goals this way, see [creating SMART goals](/articles/creating-smart-goals) and the [SMART goal steps](/articles/smart-goal-steps) walkthrough. The [SMART action plan](/articles/smart-action-plan) page shows how to turn the finished goal into a sequence of tasks.

## The 6 steps of the SMART goal approach
### Step 1: Capture the rough idea
Write the goal in everyday language, even if it's messy. "I want to get better at presentations," "I need to save money," "I should study more." Don't polish it yet. The raw version tells you what you actually want.

### Step 2: Make it specific
Answer: what will I do, and what does "done" look like? "Get better at presentations" becomes "deliver a 10-minute update at the monthly team meeting without reading from the slides." If two people would describe different results, it isn't specific yet.

### Step 3: Add a measure and a baseline
Put a number on it, and write down today's number if you can. "Save money" becomes "save 200 a month, starting from 0 in a separate account." If you can't measure it, you can't tell whether the approach is working. The [measurable goals guide](/articles/measurable-goals-guide) has ways to measure softer goals too.

### Step 4: Test achievable and relevant
Achievable: can you do this with your current time and skills, or with skills you can get in time? Relevant: does it matter enough that you'll still care in four weeks? If either answer is no, change the goal before you add a date. A shorter test lives in the [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) and [relevant goals](/articles/relevant-goals) guides.

### Step 5: Set a deadline and a checkpoint
Pick a real date, not "soon." Then add at least one checkpoint before that date, so you can adjust. "By 30 June, with a review on 15 May" is much stronger than "by the summer." See [time-bound goals](/articles/time-bound-goals) for how to choose a timeframe that fits the work.

### Step 6: Add a first action and a review habit
This is the step most people skip. Write the next physical action ("open a savings account on Monday," "book a 20-minute practice slot on Thursday") and a weekly review, even if it's only 10 minutes. The approach is not finished until both are in your calendar.

## A worked example: from wish to plan
**Rough idea:** "I want to get fitter."

- **Specific:** Walk after work, rather than "get fitter" or "join a gym someday."
- **Measurable:** 30 minutes, four days a week. Current baseline: one short walk at weekends.
- **Achievable:** Four days is a stretch from one, but the time exists after work. Seven days plus a diet overhaul would not be achievable yet.
- **Relevant:** I want more energy for the evenings with my family, and my next health check is in three months.
- **Time-bound:** Eight weeks, with a review every Sunday.

**SMART goal:** "I will walk for 30 minutes after work on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday for the next eight weeks, and log each walk, so I have more energy in the evenings."

**First action:** Put four 30-minute walks in next week's calendar, and lay out walking shoes tonight.

**Review:** Every Sunday, count the walks. If I hit four, keep going. If I hit two or fewer, drop to three days rather than quitting.

That is the SMART goal approach in one page. The same six steps work for a work project, a study target or a money goal.

## How to use the approach with a team or a class
The method is just as useful when you're helping someone else set a goal.

1. Ask them to say the rough idea out loud.
2. Work through the five criteria with questions, not corrections: "How will we know this is done?" "What's the number today?" "What might get in the way?"
3. Let them write the final sentence. People stick to goals they've worded themselves.
4. Agree the first action and the date you'll look at it again.

Managers can use this in a one-to-one in under 15 minutes. Teachers can use it as a short classroom activity. In both cases, the value is the conversation, not a perfect form.

## Where the SMART goal approach usually fails
- **Stopping at the sentence.** A well-written goal that never reaches the calendar is still a wish.
- **Too many goals at once.** The approach works best on one or two goals. Five "SMART" goals are just a longer to-do list.
- **No baseline.** Without a starting number, "improve by 20%" is guesswork.
- **A date with no checkpoint.** Problems show up late, when it's too late to adjust.
- **Treating the letters as a test to pass, not questions to answer.** Writing "this is measurable" is not the same as adding a number.
- **Never rewriting.** If week two shows the target was too big, rewrite it. That *is* the approach working.

## When this approach is the wrong tool
The SMART goal approach is designed for goals that take weeks or months and need a clear finish line. It is less useful for:

- **Tiny one-off tasks.** "Email the supplier today" does not need five criteria. Put it on a list and do it.
- **Values and identity.** "Be a more patient parent" is a direction, not a goal. Use the approach only after you pick a behaviour you can count, such as "phone-free dinners five nights a week for a month."
- **Crisis weeks.** If everything is on fire, stabilise first. Come back to structured goals when you have enough attention to review them.

Used in the right cases, though, the six steps prevent the most common failure: a goal that sounds good and never changes what you do on Tuesday.

## A one-page checklist you can reuse
Copy this and use it whenever you set a new goal:

- Rough idea written down
- Specific result described in one line
- Measure and baseline added
- Achievable and relevant checked honestly
- Deadline and mid-point review set
- First action in the calendar
- Weekly 10-minute review booked

If any box is empty, the approach isn't finished yet.

## FAQ: the SMART goal approach
### What is the SMART goal approach?
It is a practical method for turning a vague wish into a goal you can act on. You make the idea Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound, then add a first action and a review date so the goal leaves the page and enters your week.

### Is the SMART approach still useful?
Yes, if you use it as a process rather than a slogan. The five letters still catch the usual problems (no number, no date, no focus). The approach stays useful when you also break the goal into steps and review it regularly.

### How long does it take to use this method?
About 15 minutes for a personal goal, or a short one-to-one for a work goal. Most of that time is spent finding a baseline and choosing a first action, not writing a clever sentence.

### What should I do after I write the SMART goal?
Put the first action in your calendar and set a weekly review. Without those two steps, the sentence tends to be forgotten by the following week.
