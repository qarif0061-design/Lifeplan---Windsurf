Most people know the SMART acronym, but far fewer know the thinking behind it. **SMART goal setting theory** is really two ideas that grew up separately and fit together well: George Doran's practical SMART model for managers, and Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's goal setting theory from psychology. When people search for "goal setting theory SMART", they usually want to know whether the method is just a catchy checklist or whether there is real research underneath it. The short answer: SMART is a writing tool, and goal setting theory explains why well-written goals tend to work. This guide walks through both, shows where they overlap and where they differ, and gives you a simple way to apply them this week.

## Where the SMART goal theory started: George Doran (1981)

The SMART acronym first appeared in a short article by George T. Doran in the November 1981 issue of *Management Review*, titled "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives." Doran was writing for managers who struggled to turn vague company aims into objectives people could act on.

His original letters were slightly different from the version most of us learned:

- **Specific**: target a particular area for improvement.
- **Measurable**: quantify, or at least suggest, an indicator of progress.
- **Assignable**: say who will do it.
- **Realistic**: state what results can realistically be achieved with the resources available.
- **Time-related**: say when the results can be achieved.

Over the years, "Assignable" mostly became "Achievable" or "Attainable", and "Realistic" often became "Relevant". You can read more about the original article in our post on [Doran's 1981 SMART goals](/articles/doran-1981-smart-goals).

One detail people often miss: Doran said not every objective needs all five criteria. He saw SMART as a guide for clearer writing, not a rigid test. That matters, because it means the acronym was never meant to be a scientific theory on its own.

## Locke and Latham's goal setting theory

The research side comes from psychology. Edwin Locke began publishing on goals and task motivation in the 1960s, and his 1968 paper "Toward a theory of task motivation and incentives" is usually cited as the starting point. Later, Locke worked closely with Gary Latham, and in 1990 they published *A Theory of Goal Setting and Task Performance*, which pulled decades of studies into one framework.

Their central findings are easy to state:

1. **Specific, difficult goals tend to lead to better performance** than easy goals or vague instructions like "do your best."
2. **Goals work through a few clear mechanisms.** They direct attention toward what matters, increase effort, encourage persistence, and push people to find better strategies.
3. **Some conditions make goals work better.** The main ones are commitment to the goal, feedback on progress, and the complexity of the task.

Notice what is not on that list: a five-letter acronym. Goal setting theory doesn't mention SMART at all. But its core ideas line up closely with what SMART asks you to write down.

## How SMART goal setting theory fits together

Here is the practical way to think about it. Goal setting theory tells you *what makes goals effective*. SMART gives you *a format for writing goals that have those qualities*.

| Goal setting theory idea | SMART letter that supports it |
|---|---|
| Goals should be clear, not "do your best" | Specific |
| People need feedback on progress | Measurable |
| Commitment and ownership matter | Assignable / Achievable |
| Goals should stretch but stay possible | Achievable / Realistic |
| Deadlines focus attention and effort | Time-bound |

There is one real tension. Locke and Latham found that *difficult* goals often outperform easy ones, while SMART's "Achievable" and "Realistic" can tempt people to pick safe targets. The fix is to aim for goals that are hard but believable. Our guide to [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) goes deeper on finding that middle ground.

## The SMART model of goal setting in action: a worked example

Let's take a common, vague goal and run it through both lenses.

**Vague goal:** "I want to get better at public speaking."

**Step 1, make it specific (clarity).** What does "better" mean? Say it means giving a clear, confident 10-minute talk at work without reading from notes.

**Step 2, make it measurable (feedback).** Decide how you'll know. For example: record each practice talk and rate it on three things (pace, eye contact, filler words), and ask one colleague for feedback after each real talk.

**Step 3, check it is achievable but challenging.** If you have never spoken in front of a group, a keynote next month is too far. Presenting at a team meeting in six weeks is a stretch you can believe in.

**Step 4, make it relevant (commitment).** Why does this matter to you? Maybe you want to lead client meetings this year. Writing that reason down helps commitment, which goal setting theory treats as a key condition.

**Step 5, make it time-bound.** "Give a 10-minute talk without notes at the team meeting on the first Friday of next quarter, practicing twice a week until then."

**Final goal:** "By the first Friday of next quarter, I will give a 10-minute talk without notes at our team meeting. I'll practice twice a week, record each session and score it on pace, eye contact and filler words."

That goal is SMART on paper, and it also has what the research says helps: clarity, a real challenge, a reason to commit, and a built-in feedback loop. For a step-by-step version of this process, see [the SMART goal process in 5 steps](/articles/smart-goal-steps).

## SMART goal methodology: what the theory suggests you add

Goal setting theory points to a few habits that a bare SMART statement doesn't always cover.

- **Plan for feedback, not just measurement.** A number only helps if you look at it. Pick a fixed review time, such as Sunday evening, and check progress then. Our guide to [measurable goals](/articles/measurable-goals-guide) has ideas for picking metrics.
- **Break complex goals into learning goals.** For new or complicated tasks, Locke and Latham noted that pushing hard on an outcome too early can backfire. Early on, a goal like "learn three ways to open a talk" may serve you better than "get top ratings."
- **Protect commitment.** Tell someone, write down your reason, and connect the goal to something you care about.
- **Keep the number of goals small.** Attention is limited. Two or three active goals are easier to give real effort to than ten.

## Limits of SMART goal setting theory

No framework is perfect, and it helps to know where this one struggles.

- **It can encourage tunnel vision.** A narrow, measurable target can pull attention away from things that matter but are hard to measure, like quality or relationships.
- **It can make people play it safe.** As mentioned above, "realistic" can drift into "easy."
- **It fits outcomes better than exploration.** If you're still figuring out what you want, a fixed SMART goal may come too early. Start with a short experiment instead.

## FAQ

### Is SMART evidence-based?

The SMART acronym itself came from management practice, not from a lab study. Doran proposed it as a practical guide. However, the qualities SMART asks for, especially specific goals, clear measures of progress and deadlines, line up with findings from Locke and Latham's goal setting research. So SMART is best described as a practical tool that is consistent with an evidence-based theory, rather than a theory proven on its own.

### What did Locke find?

Edwin Locke, later working with Gary Latham, found that specific and challenging goals generally lead to higher performance than easy or vague goals such as "do your best." They also described how goals work (by directing attention, raising effort, increasing persistence and prompting better strategies) and what strengthens them (commitment, feedback and attention to task complexity).

### Who created the SMART goal model?

George T. Doran introduced the SMART acronym in a 1981 *Management Review* article.

### What is the difference between SMART goals and goal setting theory?

Goal setting theory is a body of research that explains why goals affect performance. SMART is a checklist for writing a goal. You can use SMART to apply the theory, but the theory is broader and also covers things like commitment and feedback.

### Should my SMART goals be hard or easy?

Hard but believable. The research favors challenging goals, and SMART reminds you to keep them achievable. Aim for a goal that stretches you while still feeling possible with effort.
