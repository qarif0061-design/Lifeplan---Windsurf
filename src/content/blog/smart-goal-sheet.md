A **SMART goal sheet** is a single page that holds everything one goal needs: what you want, how you'll measure it, why it matters, the deadline and the next step. That's it. No binder, no 12-page workbook. If a goal can't fit on one sheet, it usually isn't clear enough yet, and the act of squeezing it onto one page is what forces the clarity.

This guide gives you a copy-ready one-page SMART goal setting sheet, a filled example, a simple goal table you can use to track progress, and an honest look at whether paper or an app works better. The sheet below is the quick version you can fill in over a cup of tea.

## Why a one-page SMART goal sheet works

Most goals fail quietly. Not because people are lazy, but because the goal lives only in their head, where it shifts shape every week. "Get fit" on Monday becomes "maybe just walk more" by Thursday. A written sheet stops that drift.

A one-page format has three practical advantages:

- **It's fast to fill in.** You can finish it in ten to fifteen minutes, so you actually do it.
- **It's easy to review.** One glance on a Sunday evening tells you whether you're on track.
- **It's easy to keep visible.** A single page fits on a fridge, a desk wall or the first page of a notebook.

The SMART part (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) does the heavy lifting. If you need a refresher on each letter, our [complete guide to SMART goals](/articles/smart-goals-complete-guide) explains them in detail.

## The SMART goal sheet (copy or print this)

Copy the sheet below into a notebook, a document or a notes app. Keep the headings exactly as they are so every goal you write follows the same shape.

**Goal name:** (a short label, e.g. "Run a 5K")

**Specific: What exactly will I do?**
Write one sentence with an action verb. Who, what and where.

**Measurable: How will I know I'm done?**
A number, a count or a yes/no finish line.

**Achievable: Why is this realistic for me?**
Note the time, money and skills you already have, and what you'll need to learn.

**Relevant: Why does this matter right now?**
One line linking the goal to something bigger in your life or work.

**Time-bound: What is the deadline?**
A calendar date, plus one or two checkpoint dates in between.

**First step (this week):**
The smallest action you can take in the next seven days.

**Weekly progress:** (use the goal table below)

**Finished on:** ______

That's the whole sheet. Resist the urge to add more boxes. You can always attach extra notes, but the core page should stay short.

## A filled SMART goal sheet example

Blank forms are easy to understand and hard to fill in. Here's a realistic example for someone who wants to read more.

**Goal name:** Read 6 books by the end of March

**Specific:** I will read non-fiction and fiction books for 20 minutes every evening before bed, at home, instead of scrolling my phone.

**Measurable:** I'll finish 6 books. I'll log the title and the date I finish each one.

**Achievable:** I already read about one book a month without trying. Twenty minutes a night is roughly 10 to 15 pages for me, which gets through an average book in two to three weeks. I already own three of the books.

**Relevant:** I want to cut late-night phone use because it's hurting my sleep, and reading is the replacement habit.

**Time-bound:** Deadline March 31. Checkpoints: 2 books by January 31 and 4 books by February 28.

**First step (this week):** Put my phone charger in the kitchen and leave the first book on my pillow.

Notice that the "Specific" line does more than say "read more." It names the time, the place and the habit it replaces. That's the detail that makes a goal easy to act on.

## SMART goal table for tracking progress

The sheet tells you where you're going. A simple **smart goal table** tells you whether you're getting there. Add one row per week and keep it on the same page or the back of the sheet.

| Week | Target | Actual | On track? | Note for next week |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 7 reading sessions | 5 | Nearly | Missed two late nights; read earlier |
| Week 2 | 7 reading sessions | 7 | Yes | Finished book 1 |
| Week 3 | 7 reading sessions | 6 | Yes | Book 2 is slower; that's fine |
| Week 4 | 7 reading sessions | ... | ... | ... |

Some people prefer a **smart goal chart** instead, such as a simple bar for each week or a row of boxes you shade in as you go. Charts are more motivating at a glance, while tables are better for notes. Pick whichever you'll actually look at. The important thing is that "Actual" is filled in honestly every week.

## How to fill in your SMART goal setting sheet in 15 minutes

If you've never written a goal this way, here's a simple order that works well:

1. **Start with the measurable finish line.** Ask "what would I see, count or hold when this is done?" It's often easier to begin here than with the wording.
2. **Write the specific sentence around it.** Add the action, the time and the place.
3. **Pick the deadline, then work backwards.** Split the time into two or three checkpoints. If the checkpoints look impossible, the goal isn't achievable yet, so shrink it.
4. **Write one honest line for "relevant."** If you can't think of a reason, the goal may belong to someone else's expectations, not yours.
5. **Choose a first step you can do this week.** Make it small enough that you'd feel silly not doing it.

A quick test before you finish: read the sheet out loud to a friend. If they could tell, on the deadline, whether you succeeded or not, the goal passes.

## Paper SMART goal sheet or app: which works better?

This is the question most people ask once they've filled in their first sheet. The honest answer is that it depends on how you live day to day.

**A paper SMART goal sheet works better when:**

- You think more clearly with a pen in hand.
- You want the goal physically visible, like on a fridge or wall.
- You only have one or two goals and review them weekly.

**An app works better when:**

- You have several goals running at once.
- You want reminders, so the goal doesn't disappear for two weeks.
- You want progress tracked automatically instead of redrawing a table.
- You carry your phone everywhere but rarely carry a notebook.

Many people use both: they draft the goal on paper (because writing it slowly helps), then move it into an app for daily tracking and reminders. If you're torn, our comparison of [digital planners vs paper planners](/articles/digital-planner-vs-paper) goes deeper into the trade-offs.

## Common mistakes on a SMART goal sheet

- **Writing three goals on one sheet.** One sheet, one goal. Otherwise none of them get enough attention.
- **Leaving "Measurable" vague.** "Feel healthier" can't be checked. "Walk 8,000 steps on 5 days a week" can.
- **Skipping the first step.** A goal with no next action stays a wish. The first step is what gets you moving this week.
- **Never reviewing it.** Put a recurring 10-minute review in your calendar, the same day each week.
- **Refusing to adjust.** If week three shows the target is too big, change the sheet. Adjusting a goal on purpose is not failing.

## FAQ

### What is a SMART goal sheet?
A SMART goal sheet is a one-page form that captures a single goal using the SMART criteria: what you'll do (Specific), how you'll know it's done (Measurable), why it's realistic (Achievable), why it matters (Relevant) and when it's due (Time-bound), plus your first step.

### Paper sheet or app: which works better?
Paper is great for thinking a goal through slowly and keeping it visible. An app is better for reminders, several goals at once and automatic progress tracking. Many people draft on paper and track in an app.

### What's the difference between a SMART goal sheet and a worksheet?
A sheet is the short one-page version. A worksheet usually goes deeper, with prompts for obstacles, resources and review plans. Use a sheet for quick goals and a worksheet for bigger ones.

### How often should I review my SMART goal sheet?
Once a week works for most goals. Fill in your goal table, check whether you're on track for the next checkpoint and adjust the plan if needed.

### Can I use a SMART goal table instead of a full sheet?
A table is great for tracking, but it doesn't explain why the goal matters or what makes it realistic. Use the sheet to set the goal and the table to track it.
