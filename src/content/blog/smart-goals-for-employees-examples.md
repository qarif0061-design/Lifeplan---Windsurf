Performance review season has a familiar pattern. Your manager asks for goals, you stare at a blank form, and the best you can come up with is "do a good job" or "improve communication." Goals like that are hard to achieve and impossible to review. This guide gives you **SMART goals examples for employees** that are specific enough to use in a review, grouped by area, with advice on how to write goals that fit your own role and how to get your manager to agree to them.

A SMART goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. For employees, "relevant" is the key letter: the best goals connect your work to what your team and company need, so your manager can see why the goal matters. The examples below show how to do that.

## Why employees should write their own goals
Many people wait for their manager to hand them goals. That's a missed chance. When you draft your own goals you:

- **Shape your own role.** You choose the skills and projects you want to grow into.
- **Make reviews easier.** Evidence is collected as you go, not remembered the night before.
- **Show initiative.** Managers notice people who arrive with clear goals.
- **Reduce surprises.** Agreed goals mean no confusion at the end of the year.

## What a good employee goal looks like
Here is the difference between a weak and a strong goal:

**Weak:** "Improve my sales results."
**Strong:** "Increase my monthly sales from 40,000 to 48,000 by 30 September by making 10 extra follow-up calls a week and reviewing my pipeline every Friday."

The strong version names a starting point, a target, a date, an action and a review habit. You can check it in a one-to-one and you can tell, at any point, whether you are on track.

A simple formula to copy: *I will [action] [how much or how often], so that [result], by [date].*

## SMART goals examples for employees: performance and productivity
1. Reduce the time I take to close a support ticket from 24 hours to 18 hours on average by 31 March by using saved replies and a daily queue review.
2. Complete 95% of my assigned tasks by their due date for the next six months, tracked weekly in the team task board.
3. Cut errors in my monthly reports from an average of 5 to 1 by the end of Q2 by using a checklist before submitting each one.
4. Increase my output from 12 to 15 completed items a week by 30 June by blocking two focused hours each morning and turning off notifications.
5. Reduce the time I spend on repetitive admin from 6 hours to 3 hours a week within 8 weeks by automating two recurring tasks.

## How to write your own SMART goal in five steps
1. **Start with the problem or opportunity.** What do you want to improve, learn or achieve in your role?
2. **Find your baseline.** What is the number today (response time, sales, errors)? You can't improve what you haven't measured.
3. **Choose a target and a date.** Make it a stretch but realistic. Use [measurable goals](/articles/measurable-goals-examples-for-work) for ideas on how to count different kinds of work.
4. **Link it to a team or company goal.** This makes it relevant and easier for your manager to support.
5. **Add a review date and one first action.** Put the date in your calendar today.

For work-related goals in more depth, see [SMART goals examples for work](/articles/smart-goals-examples-for-work).

## How to get your manager to agree
- **Send a draft before the meeting.** Three to five goals is enough. People can react to a draft much faster than to a blank page.
- **Explain the "why".** One sentence on how each goal helps the team makes approval easier.
- **Ask what support you need.** Training, time, access or tools. Put it in writing.
- **Agree on how it will be measured.** Which numbers or evidence will count in the review?
- **Schedule check-ins.** A monthly 15-minute chat avoids end-of-year surprises.

## How to track goals and collect review evidence
The best review is the one where you already have the evidence. Keep a short running log:

- Each week, note one line per goal: progress, blockers, next step.
- Save positive feedback, emails and numbers in one folder as you go.
- At the end of each month, update your numbers against the target.

## Common mistakes with employee goals
- **Too many goals.** Three to five, not fifteen.
- **Goals that are just job duties.** "Answer emails" is your job. A goal should stretch or improve something.
- **No baseline.** Without a starting number, improvement can't be shown.
- **Only output goals.** Mix performance, learning and teamwork.
- **Not writing them down.** A goal that isn't recorded is forgotten by March.

## FAQ: SMART goals for employees
### What are good SMART goals for employees?
Good goals are specific, measured and dated, and linked to the team's priorities. Examples include reducing ticket response time from 24 to 18 hours, completing a certification by a set date, or mentoring a new colleague for three months.

### How many goals should an employee have?
Three to five is a good range for most roles. That's enough to cover performance, learning and teamwork without spreading your attention thin.

### How do I write goals for my performance review?
Start with your baseline numbers, choose a target and a date, link each goal to a team or company priority, and agree on how it will be measured with your manager. Then keep a short log of progress so you have evidence at review time.

### What if my job is hard to measure?
Use proxies: counts of tasks completed, turnaround time, feedback scores, number of improvements made or stakeholder ratings. Even a simple yes/no milestone ("draft approved by 31 May") makes a goal measurable.
