Managers have a double job when it comes to goals. They need goals of their own, and they need to help every person on the team set goals too. Done badly, goals become a once-a-year form that nobody reads. Done well, they give the team a clear direction, make performance reviews fair and show you early when something is slipping. This guide gives you **SMART goals for managers** with practical examples for yourself, your team and your own development, plus a simple process to set and review them.

A SMART goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. For managers, "measurable" matters even more than usual, because so much of the work (coaching, communication, culture) feels hard to count. The examples below show how to put numbers on those too.

## Why managers need SMART goals
Managers are measured on what their team achieves, but much of that is outside their direct control. That makes clear goals essential for three reasons:

1. **Alignment.** When your goals are explicit, your team can see how their work connects to the bigger target.
2. **Fairness.** Specific, measurable goals reduce bias in reviews. "Handled 40 tickets a week at 95% satisfaction" is easier to discuss than "seems busy."
3. **Early warning.** Time-bound milestones show problems in week three, not month eleven.

For a broader overview, see the main [SMART goals for managers](/articles/smart-goals-for-managers) pillar, which covers role types and templates.

## Three kinds of goals every manager needs
It helps to separate your goals into three groups, so you don't focus only on delivery:

- **Team performance goals:** what the team delivers.
- **People goals:** how you develop and support individuals.
- **Personal development goals:** how you grow as a manager.

Aim for two or three goals in each group at most. Seven or eight in total is already a lot.

## SMART goals for managers: team performance examples
1. Reduce average project delivery time from 12 weeks to 10 weeks by the end of Q3 by introducing a weekly planning meeting and removing two unnecessary approval steps.
2. Increase the team's on-time delivery rate from 78% to 90% by 30 June, tracked monthly on the project dashboard.
3. Cut rework on completed tasks by 25% within six months by adding a short checklist review before hand-off, measured by the number of reopened tickets.
4. Raise customer satisfaction for my team from 4.1 to 4.5 out of 5 by the end of the year by reviewing the five lowest-rated cases with the team each month.
5. Hit the quarterly budget within 3% of plan for the next four quarters by reviewing spending against budget on the first Monday of each month.

## SMART goals for managers: communication and culture examples
Culture feels soft, but you can still measure behaviours that shape it.

11. Run a 15-minute team stand-up three times a week for the next quarter and send a short written summary of decisions afterwards each Friday.
12. Increase the team's engagement survey score for "I understand how my work contributes to company goals" from 3.6 to 4.2 by the next survey in September.
13. Share the team's top three priorities in writing at the start of every month and review them in the monthly meeting, for the next six months.
14. Collect anonymous feedback on my management from the team twice a year, and publish two actions I'll take in response each time.
15. Run one team retrospective after every major project for the next 12 months, with at least two improvements implemented each time.

## Personal development goals for managers
Managers often develop their team and forget themselves. A few examples:

16. Complete a leadership or management programme by 30 September and apply one tool from it at every team meeting for the following quarter.
17. Learn the basics of budgeting and forecasting by completing an online course by 31 March, then prepare next year's team budget with minimal help from finance.
18. Improve my delegation by handing over at least two recurring tasks to team members each month for the next six months, and review the outcomes with them.
19. Find a mentor outside my team and meet monthly for the next 12 months, with one specific topic for each meeting.
20. Read one book on management per quarter and share one lesson with my team afterwards.

## How to set SMART goals with your team
Managers get better results when goals are set *with* people, not *for* them. A simple five-step process:

1. **Start with the team goal.** Share what the team needs to achieve this quarter or year, and why.
2. **Ask each person to draft their own goals.** People commit more to goals they've written themselves. Give them the five SMART questions as a checklist.
3. **Review together.** Check each goal for clarity, numbers and dates. Use a quick [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) formula to rewrite weak ones.
4. **Agree on support.** For each goal, note what you will do: training, access, time, removing blockers.
5. **Set review dates.** Put the monthly or quarterly check-ins in the calendar straight away.

## How to track goals without drowning in admin
Tracking should take minutes, not hours. A rhythm that works for most teams:

- **Weekly:** a five-minute check in the team meeting: "What moved this week? What's blocking?"
- **Monthly:** a short review with each person on progress against numbers.
- **Quarterly:** a deeper conversation to adjust or replace goals that no longer fit.

## Common mistakes managers make with goals
- **Too many goals.** Three to five per person is plenty. More dilutes focus.
- **Goals that measure activity, not results.** "Send 20 emails" is activity. "Book 5 meetings" is a result.
- **Cascading goals without discussion.** If people don't understand why, they won't own it.
- **Forgetting a baseline.** You can't say "improve by 20%" if you don't know today's number.
- **No follow-up.** A goal not discussed for three months is a goal that's dead.

## FAQ: SMART goals for managers
### What are good SMART goals for a manager?
Good manager goals cover three areas: team performance (delivery, quality, budget), people (one-to-ones, development plans, retention) and personal growth (training, delegation, mentoring). Each should have a number and a date, such as "raise on-time delivery from 78% to 90% by 30 June."

### How many goals should a manager have?
Between five and eight in total, split across team performance, people and personal development. More than that makes it hard to give each one real attention.

### How do I set goals for my team members?
Share the team's priorities first, then ask each person to draft two or three goals. Review them together using the five SMART questions, agree on what support you'll provide and set check-in dates.

### How often should managers review goals?
A quick weekly check, a monthly one-to-one on numbers and a deeper quarterly review work well for most teams. Adjust goals if circumstances change.
