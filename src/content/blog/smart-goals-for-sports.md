"Get better at football" or "get fitter before the season" are goals almost every athlete has said out loud, and they rarely change what happens at training. They are too vague to act on and impossible to check. **SMART goals for sports** fix that. If you have searched for "smart goals sport" ideas, the idea is simple: turn the wish into a goal that is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. "Get faster" becomes "Cut my 5 km time from 27:30 to 26:00 by the club race on 15 March by doing two interval sessions and one long run each week." In this guide you'll get a simple formula, examples for team and individual sports, SMART fitness goals for beginners, and a weekly way to track progress.

## Why SMART goals for sports work better than "train harder"
Sport already has a lot of numbers: times, scores, reps, minutes played. The trouble is that most athletes only look at the result on match day, not the habits that create it. A SMART goal links the two.

- **Specific** tells you exactly what skill or quality you're improving: first-serve percentage, sprint speed, free-throw shooting, bowling accuracy.
- **Measurable** gives you a number you can check every week, so you know if training is working.
- **Achievable** keeps the target realistic for your current level, schedule and recovery.
- **Relevant** makes sure the goal actually helps your position, event or season plan.
- **Time-bound** attaches a date, usually a match, race, trial or the end of a training block.

Athletes and coaches often split goals into three types, and SMART works for all of them:

1. **Outcome goals**: win the league, make the first team, qualify for a final. You don't fully control these.
2. **Performance goals**: run a personal best, hit 70% of first serves. These depend mostly on you.
3. **Process goals**: the daily and weekly actions, like 100 extra free throws after practice. You control these completely.

The best plans have one outcome goal, one or two performance goals, and a few process goals that make the performance goals likely.

## A simple SMART goal formula for athletes
Use this sentence and fill in the blanks:

**"I will [improve this skill or measure] from [current level] to [target] by [date or event], by doing [specific training actions] [how often]."**

Example: "I will improve my free-throw percentage from 60% to 75% by the end of the regular season by shooting 50 free throws after every practice and logging my makes."

Before you commit, check it against each letter. Can you measure it this week? Is the target a stretch but not a fantasy? Does it matter for your role? If you can't answer yes to all five, adjust it. ## SMART goals for team sports (examples)
**Football (soccer)**
- "Complete at least 80% of my passes in each league match until the winter break, tracked by the coach's match sheet, by doing 15 minutes of passing drills with my weak foot three times a week."
- "Improve my Yo-Yo test level by one stage before pre-season testing on 1 August by adding two conditioning sessions a week."

**Basketball**
- "Raise my free-throw percentage from 60% to 75% by the end of the season by shooting 50 free throws after every practice."
- "Reduce my turnovers to two or fewer per game over the next eight games by doing ball-handling drills for 10 minutes every day."

**Cricket**
- "Bowl at least four of every six deliveries on a good length in nets over the next six weeks, scored by a teammate, by doing three target-bowling sessions a week."
- "Face 200 throw-downs against short-pitched bowling each week until the season opener to cut my dismissals to the short ball."

**Volleyball or netball**
- "Get 85% of my serves in over the next ten matches by serving 40 balls at the end of each training session."

Notice that each example names a skill, a number, a habit and a deadline. That's what makes them usable at training tomorrow.

## SMART goals for individual sports (examples)
**Running**
- "Run a half marathon under 2 hours on 20 April by following a 12-week plan with three runs a week and a long run that builds to 18 km."
- "Run 5 km without walking by the end of next month by doing a run-walk plan three times a week."

**Swimming**
- "Take 3 seconds off my 100 m freestyle by the regional gala on 10 May by doing two technique sessions and one sprint set every week."

**Tennis or badminton**
- "Raise my first-serve percentage from 50% to 65% in match play by the end of the summer by practising 100 serves twice a week."

**Gym and strength sports**
- "Increase my squat one-rep max from 80 kg to 95 kg in 12 weeks by squatting twice a week with a progressive plan and logging every session."

**Martial arts**
- "Pass my next belt grading in June by attending three classes a week and practising my grading pattern for 15 minutes on non-class days."

## SMART fitness goals for beginners
You don't have to play competitive sport to use SMART goals. If you're starting from scratch, keep the targets small and focus on habits first:

- "Walk 8,000 steps a day, five days a week, for the next four weeks, tracked on my phone."
- "Do three 30-minute workouts a week for eight weeks, ticking each one off in my planner."
- "Hold a plank for 60 seconds by the end of next month by practising five days a week."
- "Stretch for 10 minutes after every workout for the next 30 days to improve hamstring flexibility."

Beginners often set targets that are too big, burn out in week three and quit. Our guide to [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) explains how to set a stretch that still feels possible. ## How to track SMART sports goals week by week
A goal only works if you check it. Here is a simple weekly routine that takes about 10 minutes:

1. **Log every session.** Write down what you did: distance, reps, shots made, minutes. Keep it short.
2. **Check your number once a week.** Pick the same day, such as Sunday evening. Compare this week's measure with last week's.
3. **Review your process goals.** Did you do the extra sessions you planned? If not, why? Fix the reason, not just the number.
4. **Adjust if needed.** If you're far ahead, raise the target. If you're injured or the schedule changed, move the date rather than abandoning the goal.
5. **Plan the next week.** Put next week's sessions in your calendar so they're not left to chance.

## Common mistakes athletes make with SMART goals
- **Only setting outcome goals.** "Win the cup" depends on opponents, referees and luck. Pair it with performance and process goals you control.
- **Too many goals at once.** Pick one main goal per training block. Three goals done well beat ten forgotten ones.
- **Ignoring recovery.** A goal that needs training every single day often ends in injury. Build rest days into the plan.
- **No deadline.** "Sometime this season" means never. Tie the goal to a match, race or test date.
- **Not writing it down.** A goal you only think about is easy to quietly drop. Write it where you'll see it.

## FAQ: SMART goals for sports
**How do athletes set SMART goals?**
Start with the season or event you're aiming for, then pick one skill or fitness measure that would make the biggest difference. Write down your current level, choose a realistic target, set a deadline tied to a match or race, and list the training actions you'll repeat each week. Review the number weekly and adjust the plan if you're ahead or behind.

**What is an example of a SMART goal in sport?**
"I will improve my free-throw percentage from 60% to 75% by the end of the regular season by shooting 50 free throws after every practice and logging my makes." It is specific (free throws), measurable (percentage), achievable (a 15-point gain over a season), relevant (it helps a basketball player score), and time-bound (end of season).

**What is a SMART fitness goal?**
It's a fitness target written with the five SMART parts, for example "Run 5 km without walking by the end of next month by doing a run-walk plan three times a week." It names the activity, a clear finish line and a date.

**Should young athletes use SMART goals?**
Yes. Keep the goals simple, focus on effort and skill (process goals) rather than winning, and let the young athlete help choose the goal so they care about it.

**How many sports goals should I set at once?**
One main goal per training block, supported by two or three process goals, is plenty for most athletes. When you reach it, set the next one.
