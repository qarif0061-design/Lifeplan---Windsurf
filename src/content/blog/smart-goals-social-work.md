Goal setting sits at the centre of social work. A care plan, a case plan or a service agreement is only useful if the goals in it are clear, realistic and agreed with the person they are for. Vague goals such as "improve family relationships" or "engage with services" are hard to act on and hard to review. This guide shows how to use **SMART goals in social work**, with example goals for clients and for your own professional development, and how to write them in a way that is collaborative, strengths-based and respectful.

The examples are illustrations of structure, not clinical or legal advice. Always follow your agency's policies, your professional code of practice and the specific needs and consent of the person you are supporting.

## Why SMART goals work in social work
SMART stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. In a social work setting they help in four ways:

1. **Clarity.** Everyone involved (client, family, colleagues, partner agencies) knows what's being worked toward.
2. **Collaboration.** Writing the goal together means the person owns it.
3. **Progress tracking.** Small, measurable steps show change that would otherwise be invisible.
4. **Accountability and review.** Dated goals make multi-agency meetings and reviews more productive.

SMART should be a tool for conversation, not a form to fill in. The best goals sound like the person's own words.

## How to write client goals that people actually own
Some principles help social workers write goals that work in practice:

- **Start with the person's priorities.** Ask "what would you like to be different?" before you write anything.
- **Use strengths.** Build goals on what the person already does well.
- **Keep goals small.** One or two goals at a time are more realistic than ten.
- **Use plain language.** Avoid jargon, so the goal makes sense to the person.
- **Agree support.** For each goal, note what the person will do and what you or others will do.
- **Respect capacity and choice.** Goals must fit the person's circumstances, health and legal context.

The [SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement) guide has a simple formula you can adapt: *[Who] will [do what], [how often], by [when], with [this support].*

## SMART goals in social work: examples for wellbeing and daily living
These examples are written in the first person as a service user might say them, with support noted. Adapt the wording to the person.

1. "I will go for a 20-minute walk three times a week for the next six weeks, with a weekly phone check-in with my support worker, to help my mood and sleep."
2. "I will keep a simple daily diary of my mood for four weeks and bring it to my next appointment so we can see what helps."
3. "I will go to bed by 11 pm on five nights a week for the next month, and review how I feel with my key worker on Fridays."
4. "I will join one local community group by the end of next month, and my support worker will help me find one and go to the first session with me."
5. "I will cook a simple meal at home four evenings a week for the next eight weeks, with a weekly shopping list agreed with my support worker."

For goals that focus on emotional health, see [SMART goals for mental health](/articles/smart-goals-for-mental-health) and [SMART goals for anxiety](/articles/smart-goals-for-anxiety-examples).

## SMART goals in social work: examples for housing, money and practical stability
6. "I will attend my housing appointment on 14 March, bringing the documents on our checklist, with my worker helping me prepare the list the week before."
7. "I will open a basic bank account by 30 April, with support to book an appointment and gather ID."
8. "I will make a budget with my support worker by the end of this month and review it every two weeks for three months."
9. "I will pay a fixed amount toward my rent arrears every month for six months, agreed with my landlord, with reminders from my worker before each payment."
10. "I will complete the benefits application form by 20 March, with two sessions of support from my case worker."

## SMART goals in social work: examples for children, young people and families
For work with families, goals should involve everyone and focus on safety and strengths.

11. "Our family will eat dinner together at the table at least four times a week for the next eight weeks, and we'll talk about how it's going at each review meeting."
12. "I will make sure my child gets to school on time at least 90% of days this term, with a morning routine chart and a weekly call from the school."
13. "I will attend all of my child's review meetings over the next six months, with transport support arranged by my worker."
14. "I will attend a parenting group once a week for eight weeks, and my worker will check in after each session."
15. "I will spend 20 minutes of one-to-one time with each of my children every day for the next month, and note it on a simple chart."

## SMART goals in social work: examples for behaviour change and recovery
Goals here should be set with care and, where relevant, with specialist input.

16. "I will attend three group sessions a week for the next 12 weeks, with my worker checking attendance every Friday."
17. "I will use a daily routine chart for four weeks and talk with my worker about triggers and coping steps at each weekly meeting."
18. "I will make contact with my support network (two named people) at least twice a week for the next two months."
19. "I will practise one coping strategy from my plan whenever I notice a trigger, noting each time in a short diary for six weeks."
20. "I will attend each scheduled appointment for the next three months, with reminder calls from my worker the day before."

## Professional development goals for social workers
Social workers also need goals for their own growth, supervision and wellbeing.

21. Complete 20 hours of continuing professional development by 31 December, including one course on a topic from my last supervision.
22. Prepare an agenda and a short reflective note for every supervision session this year (at least 10 sessions), and bring at least one case for discussion each time.
23. Reduce my overdue case notes from an average of 12 to 3 by the end of the quarter by blocking 30 minutes at the end of every day for recording.
24. Take part in one reflective practice group every month for six months.
25. Protect my wellbeing by taking my full lunch break on at least four days a week and my full annual leave this year.

## How to review SMART goals with a client
A review conversation doesn't need to be formal. Try these questions at each check-in:

- What went well since we last spoke?
- What got in the way?
- Is this still the right goal for you?
- Do we need to make it smaller, change the date or add support?
- What's the next small step?

Recording progress in the person's own words keeps the process respectful. If a goal isn't working, that's information, not failure. Change the goal, not the person. The [SMART goal tracking](/articles/smart-goal-tracking) guide has more ideas for simple tracking.

## Common mistakes with SMART goals in social work
- **Writing goals for the person instead of with them.** Ownership drives results.
- **Setting too many goals.** One or two at a time is realistic.
- **Goals that describe services, not change.** "Attend 12 sessions" is an activity. Add what you hope will be different.
- **No review date.** Dated reviews keep plans live.
- **Ignoring barriers.** Transport, childcare, health and money all affect what is achievable.
- **Using jargon.** If the person can't read and understand the goal, it isn't theirs.

## FAQ: SMART goals in social work
### How do social workers write SMART goals?
They agree a small number of goals with the person, in plain language, with a clear action, a measure, a realistic timeframe and agreed support. For example: "I will go for a 20-minute walk three times a week for six weeks, with a weekly check-in from my worker."

### What is an example of a SMART goal in social work?
"I will attend my housing appointment on 14 March with the documents on our checklist, and my worker will help me prepare the list the week before." It is specific, has a date and includes the support.

### Are SMART goals suitable for everyone?
Not always in the same form. Some people benefit from broader, less numerical goals, especially in crisis or when capacity is limited. SMART is a tool to adapt, not a rule to apply rigidly. Always follow your agency's guidance and the person's needs.

### How often should goals be reviewed?
Often enough to stay useful: weekly or fortnightly for short-term goals, and at formal reviews for longer ones. Change goals promptly if circumstances change.

Choose one goal that matters most to the person, write it together using the five SMART questions and agree the first small step and the review date.
