"Be a better leader" is one of the most common goals people write after a performance review, and one of the least useful. It gives you nothing to do on Tuesday. These **SMART leadership goals examples** turn that wish into goals that are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound, so you know which habit to practise, how you will see progress, and when you will check in. Below you'll find a short formula, 20 examples grouped by leadership skill, a way to choose the right two or three for you, and a simple monthly review.

If you want examples for every kind of role, start with the [SMART goals examples for work](/articles/smart-goals-examples-for-work) guide. This post focuses only on leading people.

## What are leadership development goals?

Leadership development goals are goals about *how you lead*, not about what your team produces. Revenue, delivery dates and output are team goals. Leadership goals are about the behaviours that make those results more likely: giving clear direction, coaching, delegating, making decisions, handling conflict and growing the people around you.

That distinction matters because it keeps the goal inside your control. You can't fully control whether your team hits a target, but you can control whether you hold a weekly one-to-one with every direct report, or whether you give feedback within two days of seeing something worth mentioning. Good SMART goals in leadership measure those controllable behaviours, then look at simple signals that the behaviour is working.

## The SMART leadership goal formula

Use this sentence pattern:

> **By [date], I will [specific leadership behaviour] [how often or how much], measured by [evidence], so that [benefit for the team].**

For example: "By the end of March, I will hold a 30-minute one-to-one with each of my five direct reports every two weeks, tracked in my calendar, so that problems surface early instead of at review time."

Every part of the SMART test is there:

- **Specific:** one-to-ones with direct reports, not "communicate more."
- **Measurable:** every two weeks, five people, logged in the calendar.
- **Achievable:** 30 minutes per person fits a normal week.
- **Relevant:** it addresses a real gap (late surprises).
- **Time-bound:** end of March, then review.

If you need a refresher on writing the sentence itself, see [how to write a SMART goal statement](/articles/smart-goal-statement).

## 20 SMART leadership goals examples

Pick the area where you have the biggest gap. Adjust the numbers to your team size and workload; the ones below are starting points, not benchmarks.

### Communication and direction

1. **Weekly priorities note.** Every Monday for the next quarter, I will send my team a short note listing the top three priorities for the week, so everyone knows what matters most without asking.
2. **Explain the "why."** For every new project I assign over the next two months, I will write two sentences on why it matters and how it links to team goals, and confirm in the kickoff that people can repeat it back.
3. **Shorter meetings.** By the end of next month, I will cut our weekly team meeting from 60 to 40 minutes by sharing a written agenda a day in advance and moving status updates to a shared document.
4. **Open-door hours.** Starting this week, I will block one hour every Thursday as open time for anyone on the team, and keep it for at least 10 of the next 12 weeks.

### Coaching and feedback

5. **Regular one-to-ones.** By the end of the quarter, I will hold a 30-minute one-to-one with each direct report every two weeks, with no more than one cancellation per person.
6. **Timely feedback.** For the next 60 days, I will give specific feedback (what happened, its effect, what to keep or change) within two working days of noticing it, and note each instance in a simple log.
7. **Ask before telling.** In every one-to-one this quarter, I will ask at least two open coaching questions ("What options do you see?") before offering my own solution.
8. **Recognition habit.** Each week for three months, I will recognise one team member's specific contribution, in public or private, and track it so nobody is missed for more than a month.

### Delegation and empowerment

9. **Hand off a recurring task.** By the end of next month, I will fully delegate one recurring task I currently do myself (such as the weekly report), with a written checklist and two shadowing sessions.
10. **Decision rights.** By the end of the quarter, I will agree with each team member on which decisions they can make alone, which need a quick check, and which need my sign-off, and write it down.
11. **Stretch assignments.** Within the next six months, I will give each direct report one stretch project linked to their development plan, with check-ins every two weeks.
12. **Fewer approvals.** Over the next 90 days, I will reduce the number of items waiting on my approval at the end of each week to five or fewer by delegating low-risk sign-offs.

### Decision-making and strategy

13. **Decision log.** Starting now and for the rest of the quarter, I will record each significant team decision with its reason and expected outcome, then review the log monthly to learn from the results.
14. **Faster decisions.** For the next two months, I will make or delegate any decision that is blocking the team within three working days, unless I need data I clearly name.
15. **Strategy time.** Each week this quarter, I will protect a two-hour block for planning and thinking ahead, and move it rather than delete it if something urgent comes up.

### Team growth and culture

16. **Development plans.** By the end of next quarter, every person on my team will have a one-page development plan with one skill goal, agreed with them and reviewed in a one-to-one at least once.
17. **Team pulse.** At the end of each month for six months, I will run a three-question anonymous pulse survey and share one action I'm taking based on the answers.
18. **Handle conflict early.** For the next quarter, I will address any recurring tension between team members within one week of noticing it, with a private conversation first.

### Personal leadership growth

19. **Ask for upward feedback.** By the end of next month, I will ask three people (a direct report, a peer and my manager) for one thing I should start and one thing I should stop, and choose one change to act on.
20. **Learn a skill on purpose.** Over the next 12 weeks, I will read one leadership book or complete one short course, and apply one idea in a real situation each fortnight, writing a two-line note on what happened.

Those last two are also good **SMART leadership objectives examples** to put in a formal development plan, because they are easy to evidence at review time.

## How to choose your SMART goals in leadership

Twenty goals is a menu, not a plan. Trying to change everything at once usually means nothing sticks. A practical approach:

1. **Collect evidence.** Look at recent feedback, review notes or exit conversations. What came up more than once?
2. **Pick one behaviour goal and one growth goal.** For example, number 5 (regular one-to-ones) plus number 19 (upward feedback). Add a third only if the first two feel easy after a month.
3. **Make the measure visible.** A calendar series, a checklist or a simple log is enough. If you can't see it, you won't notice when it slips.
4. **Tell your team.** Saying "I'm working on giving feedback faster, tell me if I'm not" makes the goal more real and invites help.

**Example in practice:** Sara was promoted from senior developer to team lead of six people. Her first review said she "jumps in and fixes things herself." Instead of "delegate more," she wrote: "By 30 April, I will delegate the weekly release checklist and code-review rota to two team members, with a written guide and two shadowing sessions each, so I spend at least four extra hours a week on planning." She tracked the hours she spent on hands-on work each Friday. That turned a vague criticism into something she could do and check.

## Reviewing your leadership goals each month

A goal you only look at during annual reviews is a goal you will forget. Set a 20-minute monthly check-in with yourself:

- **Did I do the behaviour?** Count it: how many one-to-ones held, how many feedback notes logged.
- **Is it having an effect?** Look for signals: fewer late surprises, quicker decisions, people bringing problems earlier.
- **What got in the way?** Busy weeks, unclear priorities, your own habits?
- **Keep, adjust or replace?** If a goal has become automatic, swap it for the next one on your list.

## Common mistakes with leadership goals

- **Measuring the team instead of yourself.** "My team will hit 100% of targets" is a team goal. Ask what *you* will do differently to help.
- **Goals with no frequency.** "Give more feedback" can't be checked. "Within two working days" can.
- **Too many at once.** Two or three focused goals beat ten half-done ones.
- **No end date.** Without a review date, the goal drifts. Use a quarter or 90 days, then decide again.

## FAQ: SMART leadership goals

**What are leadership development goals?**
They are goals that improve how you lead people: communication, coaching, delegation, decision-making, handling conflict and developing your team. Unlike output targets, they focus on behaviours you control, with simple evidence that you are doing them.

**What is an example of a SMART goal for leadership?**
"By the end of the quarter, I will hold a 30-minute one-to-one with each direct report every two weeks, tracked in my calendar, to catch problems early." It is specific, measurable, realistic, relevant and has a deadline.

**How many leadership goals should I set at once?**
Two or three is usually enough: one everyday behaviour (like feedback or one-to-ones) and one growth goal (like asking for upward feedback). Add more only when the first ones have become habits.

**How do I measure a soft skill like communication?**
Measure the behaviour and look for signals. Count weekly priority notes sent or meetings with agendas, then watch for fewer repeated questions or clearer handoffs. Short pulse surveys can add your team's view.

**Are SMART goals in leadership different for new managers?**
The formula is the same, but new managers often benefit most from basics: regular one-to-ones, delegation and timely feedback. Experienced leaders tend to focus more on strategy time, developing future leaders and decision-making.
