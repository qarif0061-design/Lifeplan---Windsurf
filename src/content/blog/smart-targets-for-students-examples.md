Looking for **examples of SMART targets for students**? You're in the right place. A SMART target is a goal that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. For a student, that means swapping "I'll do better in maths" for something like "I'll raise my maths quiz average from 62% to 72% by the end of term by doing 20 minutes of practice questions four evenings a week."

Below you'll find a simple formula, 40 student SMART goals examples grouped by area, and tips for teachers and parents who help students set them. ## What makes a good SMART target for a student?

Most student goals fail for the same reason: they describe a feeling, not an action. "Be more organised" or "study harder" can't be checked, so nobody knows if they worked. A good SMART objective for a student answers five questions:

- **Specific:** What exactly will I do, in which subject or area?
- **Measurable:** What number will show progress (marks, pages, minutes, sessions)?
- **Achievable:** Is it a stretch from where I am now, not a leap?
- **Relevant:** Does it help with something I actually care about, like an exam, a team place or a course application?
- **Time-bound:** When will I check if I hit it?

The "achievable" part matters more for students than for most people. A target that is far above the current level usually gets dropped after a week. A smaller target that gets hit builds the confidence to set a bigger one next term.

## A simple formula for creating SMART goals for students

Use this sentence and fill in the blanks:

> **I will [action] [how often or how much] so that [measurable result] by [date].**

For example: "I will complete one past paper every Saturday so that my science mock score rises from 55% to 65% by 15 March."

Once you have the sentence, write down the baseline (where you are today) and one check-in date halfway through. That halfway check is where most students find out the plan needs a small tweak, and it's far better than finding out on results day.

## 40 examples of SMART targets for students

Change the numbers and dates to fit your own level. A target copied word for word from a list rarely sticks; one adjusted to your real starting point does.

### Exams and grades (1–8)

1. Raise my maths test average from 60% to 70% by the end of term by doing 15 practice questions four times a week.
2. Complete six full past papers for chemistry under timed conditions before the mock exam on 10 February.
3. Learn 20 new French vocabulary words each week and score at least 16/20 on Friday self-tests for eight weeks.
4. Move from a C to a B in English essays this term by planning every essay with a five-point outline before writing.
5. Finish revision notes for all 12 biology topics, one topic every three days, by 30 November.
6. Score 80% or higher on three history knowledge quizzes in a row by the end of the unit.
7. Reduce careless errors in physics calculations from about five per test to two by checking units on every answer for the next four tests.
8. Read the marking scheme for every past paper I attempt this term and write one "what examiners want" note per paper.

### Study habits and homework (9–16)

9. Hand in 100% of homework on or before the due date for the next six weeks.
10. Study for 45 focused minutes, five days a week, using a timer, and log each session in a planner for one month.
11. Start every assignment at least three days before the deadline for the rest of the semester.
12. Keep my phone in another room during homework time every school night for 30 days.
13. Review the day's class notes for 10 minutes each evening, at least four evenings a week, until the end of term.
14. Use active recall flashcards for 20 minutes a day and track the number of cards I get right each week for six weeks.
15. Ask at least one question in each maths lesson for the next four weeks to clear up anything I don't understand.
16. Organise all subject files into labelled folders this weekend, then do a 10-minute tidy every Sunday for two months.

### Reading and writing (17–22)

17. Read 20 pages of a book of my choice five days a week, finishing at least two books by the end of term.
18. Write one 300-word practice paragraph each week and get feedback from a teacher or classmate for six weeks.
19. Learn five new academic words a week and use each one correctly in my writing within the same week.
20. Improve my reading speed from 180 to 220 words per minute by doing a timed reading exercise three times a week for eight weeks.
21. Keep a reading log with one sentence of reflection per chapter for every class text this year.
22. Proofread every essay out loud before submitting it for the rest of the semester.

### Attendance, behaviour and classroom targets (23–27)

23. Arrive on time to every first lesson for the next 30 school days.
24. Reach 97% attendance by the end of term, up from 91% last term.
25. Bring all the equipment I need (books, pen, calculator) to every lesson for six weeks, checked with a packing list each night.
26. Contribute at least one answer or idea in group discussions in three lessons a week for a month.
27. Cut late homework warnings from four last term to zero this term.

### Sports, fitness and wellbeing (28–34)

28. Improve my 1 km run time from 6:00 to 5:30 by training three times a week for eight weeks.
29. Attend every football or cricket practice session this season and do 15 minutes of extra skills work after two sessions a week.
30. Sleep at least eight hours on school nights, going to bed by 10:30 pm, for four weeks and track it in a sleep log.
31. Drink a bottle of water during school and take a 10-minute walk at lunch at least four days a week for a month.
32. Do 10 minutes of stretching after every training session for the next six weeks to reduce stiffness.
33. Spend 10 minutes writing down three things that went well each Sunday evening for two months.
34. Limit social media to 60 minutes a day on school days, checked with screen-time settings, for 30 days.

### Life skills, money and future plans (35–40)

35. Save 20% of my pocket money or part-time pay each month and reach a set savings target by the end of the school year.
36. Research three college courses or career paths and write a half-page summary of each by the end of next month.
37. Build a simple portfolio with five pieces of my best work by the end of the semester.
38. Volunteer for two hours a month at a local club or charity for the next six months.
39. Cook one simple meal at home every week for eight weeks to build independence.
40. Complete one free online course in a skill I'm interested in, doing two lessons a week, within 10 weeks.

## How teachers and parents can help students set SMART targets

Students write better targets when they set them, not when targets are handed to them. A few things make a big difference:

- **Start from data.** Look at the last test score, attendance figure or homework record together, then agree a realistic next step.
- **Keep it to two or three targets.** One academic, one habit and one personal target is plenty for a term. Ten targets means none get attention.
- **Agree the check-in date upfront.** A five-minute review every two or three weeks keeps targets alive.
- **Praise the process.** Celebrate the eight study sessions completed, not just the final grade. Habits drive results.

## How to track your SMART objectives as a student

A target written once in September and forgotten by October is not a plan. The simplest tracking setup has three parts:

1. **A place to write the target**, with the baseline and the deadline.
2. **A weekly tick box or number**, such as sessions done, pages read or quiz score.
3. **A short review**, asking "Am I on track? What will I change next week?"

## Common mistakes when setting student goals

- **Targets that are only outcomes.** "Get an A" depends partly on the exam. Pair it with an action you control, like "two past papers a week."
- **No baseline.** "Improve my reading" means nothing without knowing where you start.
- **Deadlines that are too far away.** A one-year goal needs monthly mini-targets, or it drifts.
- **Copying a friend's targets.** Their weak subject may be your strong one.

## FAQ: SMART targets for students

### What is a good SMART goal for a student?

A good SMART goal names one clear action, a number to measure it and a deadline, and it is realistic from the student's current level. For example: "I will practise 15 algebra questions four times a week so my next test score rises from 58% to 68% by 20 December." It is specific, easy to check and clearly linked to a result the student cares about.

### What is a SMART target in school?

In school, a SMART target is a short, measurable goal agreed between a student and a teacher, often for a term or a unit. It might cover grades, homework, attendance, reading or behaviour. The point is that both the student and the teacher can see whether it was met on the review date.

### How many SMART targets should a student have?

Two or three at a time works best. One for an academic subject, one for a study habit and one personal or wellbeing target gives balance without spreading effort too thin.

### What's the difference between a SMART target and a SMART objective?

They are used almost the same way. "Target" is common in UK schools and usually means a specific number to reach, while "objective" often describes the planned outcome of a lesson or project. Both follow the same five SMART criteria.
