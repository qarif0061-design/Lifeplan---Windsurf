If you have ever wondered what **SMARTER objectives stand for**, the short answer is this: SMARTER takes the classic SMART checklist (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) and adds two letters at the end, usually **E for Evaluate** and **R for Review** (or Readjust). The first five letters help you write a good goal. The last two make sure you actually check in on it and change course when life gets in the way.

That second half matters more than it looks. Most goals do not fail on the day they are written. They fail quietly in week three, when nobody looks at them anymore. SMARTER is simply SMART with a built-in habit of looking back.

In this guide you will learn what each letter means, the common variations you will see online, how SMARTER compares with plain SMART, and how to turn one of your own goals into a SMARTER goal step by step.

## What SMARTER objectives stand for (letter by letter)

Here is the most common version of the acronym:

- **S = Specific.** What exactly do I want to achieve?
- **M = Measurable.** How will I know, in numbers or clear evidence, that I got there?
- **A = Achievable.** Can I realistically do this with my time, skills and resources?
- **R = Relevant.** Does this goal matter to my bigger plans right now?
- **T = Time-bound.** When is the deadline, and what are the checkpoints?
- **E = Evaluate.** How am I doing so far, compared with the plan?
- **R = Review (or Readjust).** What will I change based on what I learned?

### The E: Evaluate

Evaluating means comparing where you are with where you said you would be. It is not a vague "how do I feel about it?" moment. It is a quick, honest look at your measurable number.

Say your goal is "Read 12 books by December 31." At the end of April you should be around book 4. If you have finished 2, the evaluation is clear: you are behind. That is useful information, not a failure.

### The R: Review and readjust

Reviewing is what you do with the evaluation. You decide whether to keep the plan, change the method, move the deadline, or (sometimes) drop the goal because it is no longer relevant.

Using the reading example: maybe you notice you only read on weekends. A readjustment could be "Read 20 pages every weeknight before bed," which keeps the original goal but fixes the method.

## Other versions of the SMARTER acronym

You will find several versions of SMARTER, and none of them is the single "official" one. The common variations are:

- **E = Evaluated, Ethical, Exciting, Engaging or Extending.** "Ethical" is popular in workplace and healthcare settings. "Exciting" and "Engaging" focus on motivation.
- **R = Reviewed, Revised, Readjusted, Rewarded or Recorded.** "Rewarded" asks you to plan a small reward when you hit the goal. "Recorded" means writing the goal down and tracking it.
- **A = Achievable, Attainable, Agreed or Actionable.** "Agreed" shows up in team settings, where the person doing the work should sign off on the goal.

Author Michael Hyatt uses his own version in his goal-setting work: Specific, Measurable, Actionable, Risky, Time-keyed, Exciting and Relevant. It pushes you to set goals that stretch you a little. We break that model down in [SMARTER Goals by Michael Hyatt](/articles/smarter-goals-michael-hyatt).

Pick the version that fits your situation. For personal goals, Evaluate and Review is the most practical pair because it creates a routine. For a team, Agreed and Recorded can matter just as much.

## SMART vs SMARTER goals: what is the real difference?

The difference is timing.

- **SMART** is about the moment you *write* the goal. It is a quality check for the goal statement.
- **SMARTER** extends that into the weeks and months *after* you write it. It is a quality check for how you manage the goal.

A perfectly written SMART goal can still fail if you never look at it again. A SMARTER goal has its check-ins built in, so problems show up early, while there is still time to fix them.

There are other extensions too, such as [SMARTT goals](/articles/smartt-goals), which add an extra T for "tangible" or "trackable." They all share the same idea: SMART is a great start, but goals need follow-through.

## How to write a SMARTER goal in 5 steps

Here is a simple process you can use on any goal today.

1. **Write the SMART version first.** Start with a specific, measurable outcome and a deadline. Example: "Save $1,500 for an emergency fund by June 30."
2. **Set a progress marker for each checkpoint.** Divide the target over time. $1,500 over six months is $250 a month.
3. **Schedule your Evaluate dates.** Put them in your calendar now, for example the last Sunday of every month. Ten minutes is enough.
4. **Decide in advance how you will Review.** Write down two or three possible adjustments, such as "cut one subscription," "move the deadline by one month" or "lower the monthly amount and extend the timeline."
5. **Write the full SMARTER statement.** "Save $1,500 for an emergency fund by June 30 by moving $250 to savings on the 1st of each month. Evaluate progress on the last Sunday of each month and readjust the amount or deadline if I am more than $250 behind."

## SMARTER goals examples

Here are a few worked examples across different parts of life.

**Fitness**
- SMART: "Run a 5K in under 30 minutes by September 15."
- SMARTER: "Run a 5K in under 30 minutes by September 15 by running three times a week. Time a 5K every two weeks to evaluate progress, and review the training plan if my time does not improve for two checks in a row."

**Work**
- SMART: "Reduce average customer reply time from 24 hours to 8 hours by the end of Q2."
- SMARTER: "...Evaluate reply-time data every Friday with the team, and in a monthly review decide whether we need new templates, a shift change or a tool."

**Study**
- SMART: "Score at least 70% on my statistics final on May 20."
- SMARTER: "...Take one practice paper every two weeks to evaluate my score, and review which topics to focus on after each one."

**Personal growth**
- SMART: "Write 500 words in my journal five days a week for the next 8 weeks."
- SMARTER: "...Count my entries each Sunday, and if I miss more than two days in a week, change the time of day I write."

Notice the pattern. The SMARTER part is always a **date** (when you will check), a **measure** (what you will look at) and a **rule** (what triggers a change). For more ideas, browse our list of [SMART goals examples](/articles/smart-goals-examples).

## Common mistakes with SMARTER goals

- **Evaluating too rarely.** Checking once at the deadline is not evaluation, it is a post-mortem. For most personal goals, weekly or monthly works well.
- **Reviewing without changing anything.** If the numbers say you are behind and you keep the exact same plan, the review did not do its job.
- **Changing the goal every week.** Readjusting is healthy, but constantly moving the finish line hides the real problem. Change the *method* first, and the target only when you have a clear reason.
- **Not writing it down.** Check-ins you only plan in your head are easy to skip. Put them somewhere you will see them, like a calendar or a goal app.

## FAQ

### What does SMARTER stand for?
SMARTER most often stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound, Evaluate and Review (or Readjust). Other versions use Ethical or Exciting for the E, and Rewarded, Recorded or Revised for the R.

### What do the E and R in SMARTER objectives stand for?
In the most common version, E stands for Evaluate (checking your progress against the plan) and R stands for Review or Readjust (changing the plan based on what you learned).

### What is the difference between SMART and SMARTER goals?
SMART helps you write a clear, realistic goal with a deadline. SMARTER adds a follow-up routine so you regularly check progress and adjust, which helps you catch problems before the deadline.

### Is SMARTER better than SMART?
Not always. For a quick, short goal, SMART is usually enough. For goals that last weeks or months, or team goals, SMARTER is often more useful because it builds in regular check-ins.

### How often should I evaluate a SMARTER goal?
It depends on the goal's length. A good rule of thumb is weekly for goals under three months and monthly for longer goals. The important part is to put the dates in your calendar when you write the goal.
