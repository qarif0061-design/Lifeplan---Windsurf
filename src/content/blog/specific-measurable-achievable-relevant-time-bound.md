The phrase **specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound** is the long way of saying SMART. It is the five-part test behind almost every well-written goal, and each word does a specific job. Many people memorise the list but never learn how to apply each part, so their goals end up half-SMART: a clear target with no date, or a date with no number.

This guide breaks down each of the five words in plain language, shows a weak and a strong example for each, answers the common question of whether the R means *relevant* or *realistic*, and finishes with a worked goal that uses all five. If you only want the letters and their history, the [SMART goals acronym](/articles/smart-goals-acronym-stands-for) page is the short version. This is the full version.

## The five words at a glance
| Letter | Word | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| S | Specific | What exactly will I do? |
| M | Measurable | How will I know I'm making progress? |
| A | Achievable | Can I realistically do this with what I have? |
| R | Relevant | Why does this matter now? |
| T | Time-bound | By when, and when do I check in? |

Together they turn a wish into a plan. Take any one away and the goal gets weaker in a predictable way, which we'll see below.

## S: Specific
A specific goal names the exact action, outcome or behaviour. It removes the guesswork about what "done" looks like.

**Weak:** "Improve my writing."
**Strong:** "Write 500 words of my novel."

Use the five W questions to sharpen a goal: what do I want, why, who is involved, where will it happen and which resources do I need? You rarely need all five, but asking them usually exposes the vague part.

**Test:** If two people read your goal, would they describe the same finished result? If not, it isn't specific yet.

## M: Measurable
A measurable goal has a number, a count or clear evidence you can check. Without it you can't tell whether you are on track, and motivation fades because progress is invisible.

**Weak:** "Save more money."
**Strong:** "Save 300 a month."

Measures can be counts (10 calls), amounts (500 dollars), percentages (raise conversion by 15%), time (finish in under 45 minutes), or a yes/no checklist (all 4 chapters submitted). Pick a measure you can update weekly. The [measurable goals guide](/articles/measurable-goals-guide) has many ways to measure things that seem hard to count, such as confidence or communication.

**Test:** Can you write down today's number and next month's target?

## A: Achievable
An achievable goal is a stretch, not a fantasy. It should be possible with your time, skills, budget and current commitments. You want to feel slightly uncomfortable, not certain of failure.

**Weak:** "Run a marathon next month" (from zero running).
**Strong:** "Run 5 km without stopping within 9 weeks."

Some people call this letter *attainable*. Ask what you would need that you do not have yet (a skill, a tool, permission, more hours) and whether you can get it in time. If not, shrink the target or extend the date. The [achievable goals](/articles/achievable-goals) guide covers how to check this honestly. And if you've seen the word "attainable" in the framework, [measurable and attainable](/articles/measurable-attainable) explains how the two letters work together.

**Test:** What would have to be true for this to work, and is each of those things in my control?

## R: Relevant (or realistic?)
This is the letter that causes the most confusion, because two words are used for it.

- **Relevant** asks: *Does this goal matter to me, and does it fit my bigger plans?*
- **Realistic** asks: *Can I actually do it?*

Strictly speaking, "realistic" overlaps with "achievable", which is why many modern versions of the framework prefer "relevant". The original 1981 article by George Doran used *realistic* for the fourth letter, and many teachers, HR teams and coaches still do. Both versions work: the key is that you ask both questions. If you want the detail, read about [realistic in SMART](/articles/realistic-in-smart) and the [SMART realistic goals](/articles/realistic-smart) guide.

**Which should you use?** If your goals tend to be too ambitious, add the *realistic* question. If your goals tend to be random or disconnected, add the *relevant* question. In practice, ask both: "Is it possible?" and "Is it worth it?"

**Weak:** "Learn Japanese" (when you have no travel or career reason and no time).
**Strong:** "Reach basic Japanese conversation by October so I can handle my work trip to Osaka."

The [relevant goals](/articles/relevant-goals) guide goes deeper on connecting a goal to what matters.

**Test:** Finish the sentence: "This matters because..." If you can't, the goal isn't relevant yet.

## T: Time-bound
A time-bound goal has a deadline and, ideally, checkpoints along the way. A deadline creates urgency, and checkpoints let you adjust before it is too late.

**Weak:** "Eventually launch my website."
**Strong:** "Launch my website by 15 March, with the draft pages done by 15 February."

Avoid vague deadlines such as "soon" or "this year". Use a real date. For longer goals, add at least one midpoint review. The [time-bound goals](/articles/time-bound-goals) guide shows how to choose realistic timeframes.

**Test:** If you opened your calendar, could you point to the date?

## A full example using all five
**Rough goal:** "I want to be better at managing money."

Let's walk it through.

- **Specific:** Build an emergency fund in a separate savings account.
- **Measurable:** Save 250 a month, aiming for a balance of 3,000.
- **Achievable:** Monthly income covers bills with about 400 left over, so 250 is a stretch, but possible.
- **Relevant:** An emergency fund removes the stress of unexpected bills and protects my plan to move home next year.
- **Time-bound:** Reach 3,000 in 12 months, checking the balance on the first of each month.

**Final SMART goal:** "I will move 250 into a separate emergency savings account on the first day of every month for 12 months, reaching 3,000 by 31 December, because it will protect me from unexpected bills and support my move next year."

Notice how each word added something that the rough goal did not have. You can find more finished examples in the [SMART goals examples](/articles/smart-goals-examples) collection.

## Common mistakes when applying the five words
- **Treating the list as a checklist of words, not questions.** Writing "this is measurable" doesn't make it so. Add the number.
- **Skipping the baseline.** You can't judge "achievable" without knowing where you start.
- **Setting the date, then forgetting it.** A deadline without checkpoints is just a hope. Add reviews.
- **Using SMART on tasks that are too small.** A simple one-off task doesn't need all five words. Save it for goals that take weeks or months.
- **Never revising.** If the numbers prove wrong, update them. The framework is a tool, not a contract.

Some versions go further than five letters. If you are curious, [SMARTER goals](/articles/smarter-goals-meaning) adds *evaluate* and *readjust*, which are exactly the review habits above. And for the history of where the criteria came from, see the original [Doran 1981 SMART goals](/articles/doran-1981-smart-goals) paper explained.

## FAQ: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound
### What does specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound mean?
It is the five-part SMART framework for writing goals. Specific names the exact action. Measurable adds a number. Achievable checks it is possible. Relevant checks it matters. Time-bound gives a deadline.

### Is the R in SMART relevant or realistic?
Both are used. The original 1981 paper said "realistic", and many modern guides say "relevant". Ask both questions: can I do this, and does it matter? Either word works as long as you check both.

### Is it measurable and achievable, or attainable?
"Achievable" and "attainable" mean the same thing here: possible to reach given your resources. Different organisations pick different words, but the question is identical.

### Do all five parts have to be in one sentence?
Not always. You can write the goal in one sentence and keep the reasoning (why it's relevant, why it's achievable) in notes beneath it. What matters is that every part is clear, not that the goal is short.
